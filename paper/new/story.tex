\section*{What the paper is about}
A developer who sees a qubit end in the wrong state asks which lines of the program determine it. For
classical programs, program slicing answers that question. This paper asks what a quantum slice must keep
to answer it correctly, which dependence rule produces such a slice, and how the slice should be shown on the
developer's own source code.

\section*{The problem, and why it matters for comprehension}
Quantum programs are hard to comprehend. Their intermediate states cannot be inspected without disturbing
them, and a gate on one qubit can change another, as in phase kickback, where a gate changes the qubit that
controls it. Bug detectors such as LintQ and QChecker find known patterns, but many faults are wrong outputs
that no pattern describes, and a slice that tells the developer where to look is the natural aid.
Slicing has been proposed for quantum programs, but its dependence rules have never been checked against
what a quantum program computes. That matters for comprehension more than for any other use, because a
slice that hides code able to change the qubit misleads the reader, who will not look where the fault is.
And such slices look right. Reading a CNOT the classical way, with its control unchanged, gives wrong slices
for \numCpdgUnsoundPct{} of \numNPairs{} circuit--qubit pairs, and \numFdCpdgZMatchPct{} of those wrong
slices reproduce the program's outcome histogram, so a developer who checks a slice by its outcomes would
accept them.

\paragraph{Thesis.} A slice supports comprehension only if it never hides code that can change the qubits the
developer asks about. Classical rules violate this, often invisibly, and entanglement-based rules add code
that cannot matter. One rule satisfies it, the \emph{write-all rule}, under which a gate writes every qubit
it touches. It is proved sound and dependence-complete and costs milliseconds.

\section*{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Semantics.} A definition of what a slice of a set of qubits must preserve, for every input, and
of when an operation is a dependence of those qubits.
\item \textbf{One rule suffices.} Under the write-all rule the quantum dependence graph (QDG) gives slices
that are sound, with the induction mechanized in Isabelle/HOL, dependence-complete and distributive over
criteria, in at most \numCostLcMaxCritMs{}~ms per criterion. Three kinds of dependence are all a slice needs,
classical data, classical control and quantum data dependence. Entanglement is neither necessary nor
sufficient for dependence, and what looks like quantum control dependence is quantum data dependence. For
each of the \numGateSignalN{} standard two-qubit gates, dropping either qubit from the write set misses a
dependence.
\item \textbf{What the alternatives cost.} A pre-registered study on \numNPairs{} pairs and
\numRqThreeUnits{} seeded-fault units measures every alternative against the rule. All but
\numCpdgMissFeedback{} of the \numCpdgMissTotal{} dependences the classical PDG misses come from its single
choice of write set. The rules nest, the dependence set is itself a sound slice at the study's input, and the QDG adds only
\numLcExcess{} points to it, equalling it on \numNestDEqQdg{} pairs, while the QPDG's entanglement edges add
\numYzLcExcessRatio{} times as much code that cannot matter. Pruning by simulating one input makes slices
\numHTwoRelative{} smaller but correct for that input only.
\item \textbf{Delivery for developers.} \tool{} is a static slicer, which analyses the source of a program without
running it. % static-definition
It shows a slice as source lines, loop iterations, the classical lines that compute them and a
profile of each qubit, for Qiskit programs and OpenQASM~2 circuits. It traces \numScaleSOneSliced{} of
LintQ's \numScaleSOneFiles{} GitHub programs and \numQheTracedOfBuildsPct{} of the expert-written Qiskit
HumanEval programs that build a circuit, in \numScaleSOneMedianMs{}~ms per program in the median, and a
qubit's slice holds \numScaleReadMedianPct{} of a program's code lines.
\item \textbf{An honest limit.} Slices narrow where a developer looks but do not improve mutation-based fault
ranking, which motivates a study with developers.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{What is new, and what we build on}
\textbf{New.} The semantic justification of a rule as a slicing rule, with its proofs; the measurement of what
every alternative costs against it; the finding that three kinds of dependence suffice and the proposed
quantum kinds add none; the classical PDG's failure and why it is invisible to an outcome check; and the
quantum layer of \tool{}, which gives srcSlice's slice profiles quantum roles, a static trace of qubits and
loop iterations, the QDG and qubit profiles.

\textbf{Built on.} The relation itself is not new. It is the backward light cone that compilers and Qiskit's
\texttt{LightCone} pass compute, and LintQ orders operations by it to find bug patterns; we establish it as
the slicing rule rather than invent it. Locality of quantum operations (no-signaling) carries the soundness
proof. Classical slicing and the program dependence graph give the vocabulary. The QPDG of Yu and Zhao is
reimplemented as a documented baseline. srcML and srcSlice give \tool{} its source representation and its
classical lines, and Behler et al.'s work on srcML for quantum languages names the qubit slicer as future work.
Bugs4Q, MQT Bench, QASMBench, LintQ's corpus and warnings, Qiskit HumanEval and published mutation operators
supply the programs and faults.

\section*{How the sections fit together}
The introduction states the question and the thesis. A motivating example shows a classical slice that
reproduces the outcomes and hides the fault. The core section defines what a slice must keep, states the
write-all rule with its guarantees, and places every other rule relative to it. \tool{} is described next,
briefly, as the way the rule reaches a developer; its full design is the subject of a separate tool paper.
The pre-registered study design and its results follow, organized by four research questions, which rules
are sound and complete (RQ1), how much smaller a slice can safely be (RQ2), what each rule does to fault
localization (RQ3), and what each costs (RQ4). The discussion turns the results into implications for
comprehension tools. Related work, threats to validity and the conclusion close the paper.
