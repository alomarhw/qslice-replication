\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

When a qubit ends in an unexpected state, debugging a quantum program starts by identifying the statements that
influence that state. For classical programs a backward slice gives these statements~\cite{weiser1984program}, and
programmers use slices to read and debug code~\cite{weiser1982programmers, gallagher1991using}. Quantum programs need
this help even more, for two reasons. Their intermediate states cannot be inspected without disturbing them, and a gate
can change a qubit it does not target, as in phase kickback, where a controlled gate changes the phase of its control
without entangling the two qubits~\cite{nielsen2010quantum}, as Section~\ref{sec:example} shows. Quantum developers
debug by printing, drawing circuits, simulating and reading code, and report that this does not
scale~\cite{zappin2026challenges}. Bug detectors such as LintQ and QChecker find known patterns by static analysis,
which reads a program without running it, % static-definition
but many faults are wrong outputs that no pattern describes~\cite{paltenghi2024lintq, zhao2023qchecker}.

Slicing has been proposed for quantum programs~\cite{yu2025qpdg} and named as an aid to understanding
them~\cite{murillo2025roadmap}, but no proposed dependence rule, which decides what each operation reads and changes
and so which operations a slice keeps, has been checked against what a quantum program computes. For comprehension this matters especially, because a slice that hides code able to change the qubit directs its reader away from the fault,
and such slices appear correct. The classical slice of the textbook program of Fig.~\ref{fig:bv} reproduces the faulty
program's state and leaves out the faulty line. Over the \numNPairs{} circuit--qubit pairs of our study, reading a
controlled gate as an assignment that leaves its control unchanged gives a wrong slice for \numCpdgUnsoundPct{} of the
pairs, and \numFdCpdgZMatchPct{} of those reproduce the whole program's outcome histogram, so a developer who checks a
slice by its outcomes would accept them.

This paper asks what a quantum slice must keep, and which dependence rule produces it. A slice of a set of qubits must
preserve their joint state for every input state, which we call \emph{sound}, and an operation is a \emph{dependence}
of them if a change right after it can change that state. The \emph{write-all rule}, under which a gate writes, that is may change, every qubit it acts on, its controls
included, gives slices that are sound and keep every dependence, with less
extra code than the QPDG, the other such rule. On qubits its slice is the backward light cone, the set of gates that can reach these qubits, and
compilers already rely on its soundness~\cite{murali2019triq, qiskit2025}.

What has been missing is a proof that it
keeps every dependence, so that no other proposed kind of dependence can add one, and a measurement of what each
alternative loses. We give the proof and measure the losses on \numNCircuits{} benchmark circuits with an exact ground
truth, \numFamCircuits{} generated circuits, \numRqThreeUnits{} units of a seeded fault and an affected qubit, and
\numBugsFourQUnits{} real-fault units from Bugs4Q~\cite{zhao2023bugs4q}. The study was pre-registered, but its
confirmatory tests concern pruning, which removes operations from a slice to make it smaller, and the comparisons of
the other rules, added after the first results, are exploratory.

The paper makes four contributions. The first is a definition of what a slice of a set of qubits must preserve and of
dependence, which separates sound slices from slices that keep every dependence. The second is a proof that the slice
of the quantum dependence graph (QDG), defined by the write-all rule and extended along classical bits, is sound for
every input state, with the induction mechanized in Isabelle/HOL\footnote{Isabelle/HOL is an interactive proof
assistant that checks every step of a proof by machine~\cite{nipkow2002isabelle}. The theory is in the replication
package.}, and keeps every dependence, with evidence that none of Qiskit's standard two-qubit gates can safely write
fewer qubits. The third shows that classical data, classical control and quantum data dependence suffice, since
apparent quantum control dependence is quantum data dependence and entanglement is neither necessary nor sufficient for
dependence. The fourth measures the alternatives. The classical program dependence graph (PDG) fails almost entirely
through its reading of the control. The quantum program dependence graph (QPDG) of Yu and Zhao~\cite{yu2025qpdg} keeps
\numYzLcExcessRatio{} times as many operations that cannot affect the criterion as the QDG, whose slices are on average
\numLcExcess{} percentage points larger than the smallest slice that keeps every dependence, a gap that grows with the
number of qubits. Pruning drops dependences. The QDG slice contains \numRqThreeContainLc{} of the seeded faults, but
slices improve neither the ranking of mutation-based fault localization nor a language model's localization of faults.

The paper does not build a slicer for a particular language. Its definitions are stated on the circuit a program
builds, Section~\ref{sec:lines} shows how a slice reaches source lines, and computing slices at scale on the source
developers write is a separate question. Section~\ref{sec:example} works through the example of Fig.~\ref{fig:bv},
Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work, Section~\ref{sec:core} defines the semantics and the rules,
Sections~\ref{sec:design} and~\ref{sec:results} present the study, and the last three sections give the implications,
the threats to validity and the conclusion. The data, scripts, protocol and proofs are in an anonymous replication
