\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

This section places our work among six lines of research. For each line it states what others have done, how
our work relates to it and what we reuse. It closes with the gaps that remain open and with the data each
line was evaluated on, and Table~\ref{tab:related} summarizes both.

\subsection{Program slicing and srcSlice}
Weiser introduced program slicing~\cite{weiser1984program} and showed that programmers use slices when they
debug~\cite{weiser1982programmers}. Slicing extracts a computation that is scattered through a program from the statements around it,
which makes it a comprehension aid as much as a debugging one~\cite{binkley2007source}. Dependence graphs reduced slicing to graph
reachability~\cite{ottenstein1984pdg, ferrante1987pdg, horwitz1990interprocedural}, Podgurski and Clarke gave
dependence a semantic definition~\cite{podgurski1990formal}, and dynamic slicing restricts a slice to one
execution~\cite{korel1988dynamic, agrawal1990dynamic}. A backward slice answers which statements can affect a
value at a point, and a forward slice, its dual, answers which statements a given statement can
affect~\cite{tip1994a, horwitz1990interprocedural}. Surveys catalog the many kinds of slices that
followed~\cite{tip1994a, silva2012vocabulary, binkley2004a}. Probabilistic slicing preserves the distribution of the
criterion variables~\cite{hur2014slicing, amtoft2020theory, olmedo2025slicing}, and Gallagher names quantum
programs as a direction for semantically defined slicing~\cite{gallagher2025program}.

srcSlice computes slices from srcML, an XML form of source code~\cite{collard2011lightweight, collard2013srcml}. Instead of building a dependence graph, it records
for every variable a \emph{slice profile}, the lines where the variable is defined and used, the variables it flows
into and the calls it is passed to, and it computes dependences from these profiles as it slices. It slices the
whole system at once, giving the forward slice of every variable in every function rather than of one chosen
criterion, and slices the Linux kernel in about 20 minutes with accuracy comparable to
CodeSurfer~\cite{alomari2014srcslice}. General
analysis frameworks such as WALA, which also reads Python~\cite{wala}, build dependence graphs, but on a Qiskit
program every gate is a method call on one circuit object, so neither they nor srcSlice can tell which qubits an
operation depends on.

\emph{Relation and reuse.} We keep the classical notions, a criterion, a backward and a forward slice, and
dependence as the possibility of change, and define them for the reduced state of a set of qubits, a criterion
stronger than a distribution in one basis, which misses phase. Where srcSlice slices every variable, the \tool{}
approach slices every qubit. For any qubit or set of qubits it finds every line of the program that can affect
their state, the backward slice, and for any line it finds every qubit and line that line can affect, the forward
slice. It reuses srcML's representation and srcSlice's slice profiles for the classical part of a program and
adds a quantum layer, which Section~\ref{sec:approach} describes.

\subsection{srcML for quantum programs}
Behler et al.\ specify a srcML markup for OpenQASM~3, so that quantum syntax shares one representation with the
classical languages srcML already supports~\cite{behler2024supporting}. On it they build an execution-order
analysis that unrolls loops, expands gate and function calls and labels operations inside \texttt{if}
statements with their condition, and their QStatic tool uses the resulting per-qubit order to detect
iteration and recursion patterns and to suggest refactorings~\cite{behler2025static}. Their analyzer does not
yet handle \texttt{while} loops or qubits passed to functions as classical values~\cite{behler2024supporting},
and its order table marks the control of a CNOT apart from its target. Behler et al.\ name, as future work, the
adaptation of srcML's slicer to find every line that affects a given qubit~\cite{behler2025static}.

\emph{Relation and reuse.} Our work takes that step. We reuse the idea that a static execution order of
qubit operations, kept on srcML, is the basis of quantum analyses, and we add what a slicer needs on top of it,
a dependence rule with a guarantee. An order table serves pattern detection well, but read directly as a
dependence graph it treats the control as read-only, which Section~\ref{sec:core} shows to be unsound. Their
analyzer reads OpenQASM~3 and ours reads Qiskit and OpenQASM~2, so the two cover different languages.

\subsection{Dependence graphs for quantum programs}
Yu and Zhao's quantum program dependence graph (QPDG)~\cite{yu2025qpdg} extends the classical
PDG~\cite{ferrante1987pdg} with entanglement, no-cloning, temporal and measurement dependences and proposes
slicing on it. It is defined in prose with examples and has not been implemented or evaluated. Its own example
shows why validation matters. In the graph Yu and Zhao draw for their first listing, the slice of a measured
qubit omits the two gates that fix its outcome, so the slice returns 1 with probability
\numYzFigSliceProb{} where the program returns 1 with certainty. Kaul et al.\ build the quantum code property graph
(QCPG), a code property graph for Qiskit and OpenQASM programs, and query it for bugs and smells, among them superfluous operations, gates that
do not affect a measured qubit~\cite{kaul2023uniform}. Its data-flow edges run from the control of a CNOT to its target only, and the
authors state that the control is not changed, so the query can report gates whose effect reaches a measured
qubit back through a control. Kaul et al.\
demonstrate the graph on example programs and note the lack of large datasets.
In their roadmap of quantum software engineering, Murillo et al.\ describe the QPDG as an aid to
understanding programs~\cite{murillo2025roadmap}, and in their roadmap of testing and debugging, Ramalho et al.\
find dependence and data-flow analysis of circuits almost unexplored, naming only Kaul et al.~\cite{ramalho2025road}.

\emph{Relation and reuse.} We take the QPDG's question, which operations a qubit depends on, and answer it
against a semantic definition. Section~\ref{sec:rules} describes the baseline we reimplement from its prose, and
Section~\ref{sec:kinds} shows that its entanglement edges add no dependence the write-all rule misses.

\subsection{Entanglement analysis}
Static analyses approximate which qubits may be entangled, with type systems~\cite{perdrix2007types,
prost2009reasoning, yuan2022twist}, abstract interpretation~\cite{perdrix2008entanglement, yu2021abstract}, the
stabilizer formalism~\cite{honda2015stabilizer} and data-flow rules~\cite{xia2025entanglement}. ScaffCC assumes
that qubits that interact may be entangled~\cite{javadiabhari2015scaffcc}.

\emph{Relation.} These analyses answer a different question, whether two qubits may be correlated, and they are
not slicers. Section~\ref{sec:core} shows that entanglement neither implies nor is implied by dependence, so
their results cannot decide what a slice keeps.

\subsection{Light cones and compilers}
The \emph{backward light cone} of a set of qubits at the end of a circuit is the set of gates that can
affect their final state, and Section~\ref{sec:core} shows how it is computed. Compilers build gate-dependence graphs on this
principle to schedule and map circuits~\cite{murali2019triq, amy2020staq}, and QAOA analyses use it to bound the
gates that can reach an observable~\cite{farhi2014qaoa}. Quantum separation logics reason locally about the qubits
a program touches~\cite{zhou2021qsl, le2022qsl}.

Qiskit implements it as the \texttt{LightCone} transpiler pass~\cite{qiskit2025}. Given the qubits to be
measured, or a Pauli observable on them, the pass walks the circuit backward from the end, keeps the gates in the
light cone and also removes every gate that commutes with the observable, since such a gate cannot change its
expectation value. It returns a smaller circuit for estimating that observable, and Heim et al.\ likewise argue
that quantum dependences should account for commutation~\cite{heim2020quantum}. The pass works on a circuit,
not on source code, and decides commutation for the circuit's actual angles. It rejects circuits with mid-circuit measurements. Compilers also move a single-qubit gate within the idle time of its qubit
without changing the computation~\cite{smith2022timestitch}, and call the time windows of a circuit its
slices, a sense different from ours.

\emph{Relation and reuse.} The backward light cone is the relation of our write-all rule, and we do not claim
the relation as new. What these works do not provide is a definition of what a slice must preserve and a proof
that the light cone is exactly what a slice must keep, which Section~\ref{sec:core} gives. Removing commuting
gates goes further than the light cone, and Section~\ref{sec:results} measures what it costs. A pruned slice is
correct for the observable at the given angles but no longer contains every operation that could change the
qubit, so it can omit the place where a fault would be. We reuse Qiskit's \texttt{LightCone} pass as the
commutation-pruning baseline of the study and follow the light cone along classical bits and into source code,
which the pass does not do.

\subsection{Debugging tools and bug detectors}
Metwalli and Van Meter adapt slicing to circuits by splitting a circuit at barriers the developer places and
removing the qubits a part does not use, and their tool maps gates to source lines through tracebacks at run
time~\cite{metwalli2022debugging, metwalli2024testing}. Their phase tests detect a wrong phase but need
hand-designed test vectors to locate the faulty qubit. Qiskit's circuit-slicing utilities cut a circuit into consecutive parts by barriers, depth or gate
type~\cite{qiskitaddonutils}. Both use slicing in the sense of cutting a circuit into pieces, which helps a
developer inspect it piece by piece but follows no dependence. Ramalho et al.\ list slicing as a way around no-cloning and note that it grows hard to manage as circuits
grow~\cite{ramalho2025road}. Zappin et al.\ surveyed and interviewed 26 quantum developers, who debug with print statements, circuit
drawings, simulators and manual inspection of the code and report that these methods do not
scale~\cite{zappin2026challenges}. A slice shortens exactly that reading of code. Testing faces the related limit that phase faults escape computational-basis measurement~\cite{oldfield2024faster}.
Bug detectors find known patterns statically, and Chen et al.\ catalog quantum-specific code
smells~\cite{chen2023smelly}. LintQ runs CodeQL queries over Qiskit programs and orders two
operations when they share a qubit~\cite{paltenghi2024lintq}. QChecker matches patterns in the abstract syntax
tree~\cite{zhao2023qchecker}, and Q-PAC also detects known patterns~\cite{nayak2023qpac}. Bug
patterns~\cite{zhao2021bugpatterns}, Bugs4Q~\cite{zhao2023bugs4q}, mutation tools~\cite{mendiluze2021muskit,
fortunato2022qmutpy, garcia2022testing} and mutation-based fault localization~\cite{ishimoto2025mbfl} supply
faults and rankings to this field.

\emph{Relation and reuse.} Slices complement these tools. Manual slicing has no criterion, so the developer still
narrows the fault by hand, and a slice by the write-all rule generalizes the removal of unused qubits, since it
drops every qubit and operation that cannot reach the criterion. The \tool{} approach maps operations to source
lines statically. LintQ's ordering relation is the relation of the write-all rule, which confirms the rule in
practice, while LintQ uses it for patterns and makes no claim about slices. Of the 42 Bugs4Q bugs on which
QChecker was evaluated, 22 are wrong outputs that no pattern detects~\cite{zhao2023qchecker}, and a slice shows
where such a fault can be. We reuse Bugs4Q, LintQ's corpus and its
public warnings, published mutation operators and mutation-based fault localization.

\subsection{Open gaps and the data used}
Table~\ref{tab:related} sets these approaches side by side, although only srcSlice and the QPDG slice in the
classical sense. We compare them together because each line of work supplies
part of what a quantum slicer for comprehension needs and none supplies all of it. Behler et al.'s analysis and
the QCPG represent quantum source code for static analysis, the infrastructure a source-level slicer is built on.
The \texttt{LightCone} pass and the compilers compute the dependence relation, but on circuits rather than
programs. Cirquo and Qiskit's utilities give developers pieces of a circuit to inspect, without a criterion.
LintQ and QChecker analyze the same Qiskit programs statically and report source lines, and LintQ orders
operations by the relation a slicer needs. The seven property columns are what a slicer for comprehension
needs, and the table shows which of them each approach already provides.
Three gaps remain open, and each can be shown from the work above. First, no dependence rule proposed for
quantum slicing has been checked against what a quantum program computes, and the one example worked out in
detail, the QPDG's, gives a wrong slice. Second, the analyses that represent quantum source code, the QCPG and the execution-order tables
of Behler et al., read the control of a gate as unchanged, and no work has measured what that reading does to a
slice. Third, the slicing support developers have is manual and dynamic, and no
static slicer shows a qubit's slice on Qiskit source with its loop iterations.

The data differ as much as the methods. The dependence graphs were shown on examples, the srcML work on the
OpenQASM~3 examples, and the detectors on Bugs4Q and on LintQ's GitHub corpus, while no quantum slicing approach was
evaluated on real programs. We use the detectors' data where they fit, Bugs4Q for real faults and LintQ's corpus
and confirmed warnings for real programs, and add what a slicing study needs that they lack, circuits with a
simulated ground truth and seeded faults, which Section~\ref{sec:design} lists.
