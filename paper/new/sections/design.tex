\section{Study Design}\label{sec:design}

The study measures every rule of Section~\ref{sec:core} against the definitions of that section. It asks four
research questions, each a question a developer has of a slice, whether it can be trusted, how small it can safely
be, whether it holds the fault, and what it costs.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1} Which dependence rules give sound and dependence-complete slices?
\item \textbf{RQ2} How much smaller than the QDG slice can a slice safely be, and under which inputs does the extra
precision hold?
\item \textbf{RQ3} What does each rule do to fault localization, measured by whether the slice contains the fault and
by mutation-based fault localization?
\item \textbf{RQ4} What does each rule cost in time as circuits grow?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Pre-registration}
The protocol was committed before the runs reported here. It extends an earlier run whose results it reuses, so
the outcomes of H1 and H2 below were known when it was written. Every later change is a dated deviation, marked when
it was made after a result. The five confirmatory tests concern state-based pruning. Every other result, including
the classical PDG's share of wrong slices, is exploratory or descriptive, and so is everything added after the
first results. These additions are the classical PDG and \texttt{LightCone} baselines, the dependence set as a size
reference and its nesting with the rules, the symbolic-angle, flow-sensitive and read-only variants, and the check of
two-qubit gates in both directions. The protocol also pre-registered two descriptive tasks for language models, from which the most expensive model
was dropped for cost before any scored call.

\subsection{Subjects}
The rules are compared on circuits, where an exact simulation gives the ground truth. Corpus~A collects
\numSubjACount{} benchmark circuits from MQT Bench~\cite{quetschlich2023mqt}, QASMBench~\cite{li2023qasmbench} and
VeriQBench~\cite{chen2022veriqbench}, with \numSubjAMinQubits{} to \numSubjAMaxQubits{} qubits and a median of
\numSubjAMedianOps{} operations, at most \numSubjAMaxOps{}. Every qubit of every circuit is a criterion, which gives
\numNPairs{} circuit--qubit pairs, \numNDynamicPairs{} of them in dynamic circuits with mid-circuit measurement, reset
or conditioned gates. The \numSubjGenCount{} generated circuits come in families of known structure that grow in depth
and in width, with \numSubjGenMinQubits{} to \numSubjGenMaxQubits{} qubits and up to \numSubjGenMaxOps{} operations,
and \numSubjSweepCount{} width sweeps extend two of the families to \numSubjSweepMaxQubits{} qubits. Corpus~L has
\numSubjLCount{} wider circuits from the same suites, with \numSubjLMinQubits{} to \numSubjLMaxQubits{} qubits and up
to \numSubjLMaxOps{} operations, on which only some rules finish. Faults are seeded into corpus~A, and real faults
come from Bugs4Q~\cite{zhao2023bugs4q}. A unit is a fault and a qubit whose measured distribution it changes.

\subsection{Ground truth}
An independent oracle simulates each circuit exactly, branching over measurement outcomes, and agrees with Qiskit's
statevector simulation to within \numOracleErr{}. A slice preserves the criterion when the squared Uhlmann fidelity
between the criterion's reduced states under the slice and under the whole circuit is at least $1 - 10^{-6}$. The
dependences of Definition~\ref{def:dep} are measured by inserting \numInfluencePerturbations{} random unitaries after
every operation and comparing the criterion's state at \numInfluenceInputs{} inputs, $\ket{0\dots0}$ and random
product states. Because the inputs and perturbations are sampled, the measured dependence set can only miss true
dependences, never add false ones. A catalog of \numCatEntries{} hand-derived cases checks the ground truth and every
slicer before the runs.

\subsection{Slicers}
Each rule of Section~\ref{sec:rules} is implemented on circuits from its read and write sets or its pruning step.
Commutation pruning uses Qiskit's \texttt{LightCone} pass, which applies to \numLcqApplicable{} of the \numNPairs{}
pairs. It rejects the circuits with mid-circuit measurements, \numLcqNaMeasurements{} pairs, and on
\numLcqNaReorder{} it returns a circuit whose operations cannot be matched back to the original in order.
Exploratory variants make commutation pruning hold for all angles and give state-based pruning a joint criterion.

\subsection{Robustness}
A slice computed at $\ket{0\dots0}$ is rechecked, with its operations unchanged, under \numRobDraws{} random
computational-basis inputs, \numRobDraws{} random product inputs and, for circuits with rotation angles,
\numRobDraws{} random rebindings of every angle. A pair is robust if the slice preserves the criterion in every draw.

Whether a two-qubit gate can carry information from either of its qubits to the other is checked on each of the
\numGateSignalN{} two-qubit gates of Qiskit's standard library, parameterized gates at a fixed angle. For each
direction, seeded random product inputs are searched for two inputs of one qubit that leave the other qubit in
different reduced states. This is evidence for these gates, not a proof for all gates.

\subsection{Faults and fault localization}
Faults are seeded with \numMutOperators{} operators, \numMutPublished{} from Muskit~\cite{mendiluze2021muskit},
QMutPy~\cite{fortunato2022qmutpy} and García de la Barrera et al.~\cite{garcia2022testing}, and an angle perturbation
of our own. Of the \numMutants{} mutants, \numMutantsExcluded{} with an empty fault site are excluded, and
\numMutantsKilled{} change the measured distribution of at least one qubit. Each such qubit and its mutant form a
unit, \numRqThreeUnits{} in all, and for each unit we record the slice's size and whether it contains the fault site. Of the
\numBugsFourQTriaged{} entries of Bugs4Q, \numBugsFourQIncluded{} are circuit-level faults that port mechanically to
the current Qiskit release, and \numBugsFourQKilled{} of them change a qubit's measured distribution, giving
\numLinesUnits{} units. Running each
port records, for every operation, the line that appends it, which is mapped to the line of the original program, so
the slices of these units are also reported as lines of the programs their developers wrote.

Mutation-based fault localization ranks the operations of the faulty circuit by how their mutants change its
observable behavior, with the Ochiai formula, as Ishimoto et al.\ do for quantum programs~\cite{ishimoto2025mbfl}, on a
sample of \numMbflUnits{} units stratified by fault operator and including every Bugs4Q unit. We rank over the whole
circuit and within each slice and report the EXAM score, the share of ranked operations examined before the fault,
lower being better.

The two tasks for language models ask what a slice offers a model that reads a circuit. In the first, a model lists
the operations that can affect a qubit, for \numTOneItems{} items. In the second, it names the likely faulty
operation of \numTTwoItems{} units, given the whole circuit or a slice. The models are Claude Sonnet~5.5 and
Haiku~5.5 and the open Qwen2.5-Coder models with 32 and 1.5 billion parameters.

\subsection{Cost}
RQ4 times the rules on the generated family that grows in depth at a width of eight qubits, on width sweeps of two
generated families from \numSubjSweepMinQubits{} to \numSubjSweepMaxQubits{} qubits, and on corpus~L, each circuit in
a fresh process with limits of \numCostWallLimit{}~s and 4~GiB.

\subsection{Statistics}
Five confirmatory hypotheses compare state-based pruning with the other rules. H1 states that it is wrong on fewer
pairs than def-use, H2 that its slices are smaller than QDG slices, and H3 that at least 95\% of pairs keep a correct
slice under random product inputs. H4 states that it loses at most five percentage points of fault containment against
the QDG slice, and H5 that its slices are smaller where both contain the fault. State-based pruning starts from the
QDG slice and checks every removal at $\ket{0\dots0}$, so H1 and the directions of H2 and H5 hold by construction, and
H3 and H4 are the real tests. We use exact McNemar tests on paired outcomes and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on paired
sizes, with Holm's correction across the five. Effect sizes come with bootstrap 95\% intervals and Wilson intervals
for proportions, and exploratory intervals resample whole algorithm families. Everything else is descriptive.
