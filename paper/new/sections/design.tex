\section{Study Design}\label{sec:design}

The study measures every rule of Section~\ref{sec:core} against its definitions, asking four questions a developer
has of a slice.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1} Which dependence rules give sound and dependence-complete slices?
\item \textbf{RQ2} How much smaller than the QDG slice can a slice safely be, and under which inputs?
\item \textbf{RQ3} What does each rule do to fault localization?
\item \textbf{RQ4} What does each rule cost in time as circuits grow?
\end{itemize}

RQ1 checks Theorem~\ref{thm:sound} and Proposition~\ref{prop:complete} against measured dependences, RQ2 asks what
precision beyond the QDG slice is safe, RQ3 whether slices help find faults, and RQ4 what they cost.

\subsection{Pre-registration}
The protocol was committed before the runs reported here. It extends an earlier run whose results it reuses, so the
outcomes of two of the five hypotheses of Section~\ref{sec:stats} were known when it was written, and every later change is a dated deviation. It was
written for a slicer that prunes by state, and its research questions are worded here for the comparison of rules,
with hypotheses and measures unchanged. The five confirmatory tests concern state-based pruning. Every other result is exploratory or descriptive,
including everything added after the first results. These additions are the classical PDG and
\texttt{LightCone} baselines with the classical PDG's share of wrong slices, the dependence set and its nesting with
the rules, the symbolic-angle, flow-sensitive and read-only variants, and the gate check. Two descriptive tasks for language models were pre-registered, and the most
expensive model was dropped for cost before any scored call.

\subsection{Subjects}
The rules are compared on circuits, where exact simulation gives the ground truth. Corpus~A collects
\numSubjACount{} benchmark circuits from MQT Bench~\cite{quetschlich2023mqt}, QASMBench~\cite{li2023qasmbench} and
VeriQBench~\cite{chen2022veriqbench}, with \numSubjAMinQubits{} to \numSubjAMaxQubits{} qubits and a median of
\numSubjAMedianOps{} operations, at most \numSubjAMaxOps{}. Every qubit of every circuit is a criterion, which gives
\numNPairs{} circuit--qubit pairs, \numNDynamicPairs{} of them in dynamic circuits with mid-circuit measurement, reset
or conditioned gates. The \numSubjGenCount{} generated circuits come in families of known structure that grow in depth
and in width, the number of qubits, with \numSubjGenMinQubits{} to \numSubjGenMaxQubits{} qubits and up to \numSubjGenMaxOps{} operations,
and \numSubjSweepCount{} width sweeps extend two of the families to \numSubjSweepMaxQubits{} qubits. Corpus~L has
\numSubjLCount{} wider circuits from the same suites, with \numSubjLMinQubits{} to \numSubjLMaxQubits{} qubits and up
to \numSubjLMaxOps{} operations, on which only some rules finish. Faults are seeded into corpus~A, and real faults
come from Bugs4Q~\cite{zhao2023bugs4q}.

\subsection{Ground truth}
An independent reference simulator computes each circuit's states exactly, branching over measurement outcomes, and agrees with Qiskit's
simulation to within \numOracleErr{}. A slice preserves the criterion when the squared Uhlmann fidelity of the
criterion's reduced states under the slice and the whole circuit is at least $1 - 10^{-6}$. The
dependences of Definition~\ref{def:dep} are measured by inserting \numInfluencePerturbations{} random unitaries after
every operation and comparing the criterion's state at \numInfluenceInputs{} inputs, $\ket{0\dots0}$ and random
product states, in which every qubit starts in its own random state. A catalog of \numCatEntries{} hand-derived cases checks the ground truth and every
slicer before the runs.

\subsection{Slicers}
The study builds every rule as one graph over a circuit's operations, so that the rules differ only in their edges or
its pruning step, and slices by backward reachability. The QDG slice is also computed by the one-pass algorithm of
Section~\ref{sec:core}, which gives the same slice on all \numOnePassEqual{} pairs, and its forward pass gives the graph's forward slice for all \numOnePassForwardEqual{} operations of corpus~A, an exploratory check. Qiskit's \texttt{LightCone}
pass applies to \numLcqApplicable{} of the \numNPairs{} pairs. It refuses an observable on circuits that contain
measurements, \numLcqNaMeasurements{} pairs, and on \numLcqNaReorder{} its output cannot be matched back to the original.
Exploratory variants make commutation pruning hold for all angles and give state-based pruning a joint criterion.

\subsection{Robustness, noise and two-qubit gates}
A slice computed at $\ket{0\dots0}$ is rechecked, with its operations unchanged, under \numRobDraws{} random
computational-basis inputs, \numRobDraws{} random product inputs and, for circuits with rotation angles,
\numRobDraws{} random rebindings of every angle. A pair is robust if the slice preserves the criterion in every draw. The noise analysis, pre-registered and
descriptive, transpiles the whole circuit and each slice to single-qubit gates and CNOTs, adds depolarizing noise, which replaces a qubit's state by a random one with
a small probability, to every gate at two-qubit rates of $10^{-3}$ to $3\cdot10^{-2}$ and a tenth of that on single-qubit gates, simulates the
corpus-A pairs of at most \numNoiseMaxQubits{} qubits exactly, and compares the criterion's noisy states by trace
distance, which measures how distinguishable two states are.

The gate check takes \numGateSignalN{} standard two-qubit gates of Qiskit, parameterized gates at a fixed angle. For
each direction it searches seeded random product inputs for two inputs of one qubit that leave the other in different
reduced states. This is evidence for these gates, not a proof for all gates.

\subsection{Faults and fault localization}
Faults are seeded with \numMutOperators{} operators, \numMutPublished{} from Muskit~\cite{mendiluze2021muskit},
QMutPy~\cite{fortunato2022qmutpy} and García de la Barrera et al.~\cite{garcia2022testing}, and an angle perturbation
of our own. Of the \numMutants{} mutants, \numMutantsExcluded{} with an empty fault site are excluded, and
\numMutantsKilled{} change the measured distribution of at least one qubit. Each such qubit and its mutant form a
unit, \numRqThreeUnits{} in all, for which we record each slice's size and whether it contains the fault site, the operations the mutation changed or, for a deletion,
those next to it. Of the
\numBugsFourQTriaged{} entries of Bugs4Q, \numBugsFourQIncluded{} are circuit-level faults that port mechanically to
the current Qiskit release, and \numBugsFourQKilled{} of them change a qubit's measured distribution, giving
\numBugsFourQUnits{} units, \numLinesUnits{} of whose faulty lines map to the original program. Running each port
records the line that appends each operation, mapped to the original program, so these slices are also reported as
lines of the programs their developers wrote.

Mutation-based fault localization, which Ishimoto et al.\ evaluate for quantum programs~\cite{ishimoto2025mbfl},
ranks the operations of the faulty circuit by how their mutants change its observable behavior, here with the Ochiai
formula, a standard suspiciousness score, on a sample of \numMbflUnits{} units stratified by fault operator and including every Bugs4Q unit. We rank over the whole
circuit and within each slice and report the EXAM score, the share of ranked operations examined before the fault,
lower being better. For H5, the inspection cost of a slice is its size together with the same score for the slice read
from its last operation backward.

\subsection{Language models}
In two descriptive tasks, a model lists the operations that can affect a qubit for \numTOneItems{} items, and
names the likely faulty operation of \numTTwoItems{} units given the whole circuit or a slice. The models are Claude
Sonnet~5.5, Claude Haiku~5.5, and Qwen2.5-Coder at 32 and at 1.5 billion parameters.

\subsection{Cost}
RQ4 times the QDG slice by the one-pass algorithm from the circuit, and the other rules as built, on the generated family that grows in depth at \numCostDepthWidth{} qubits, on the width sweeps and on
corpus~L, each circuit in a fresh process limited to \numCostWallLimit{}~s and \numCostMemLimit{}~GiB. All times are wall-clock
on one machine, an Intel Xeon W-2140B at 3.2~GHz with 32~GB of memory, with Python~3.12 and Qiskit~2.3. The QDG slice is timed both by the one-pass algorithm, from the circuit in memory without a graph, and as the
construction of its graph, which compares with the construction of the QPDG's graph, and state-based pruning is timed
end to end.

\subsection{Statistics}\label{sec:stats}
Five confirmatory hypotheses concern state-based pruning. It is wrong on fewer pairs than def-use (H1), its slices
are smaller than QDG slices (H2), at least 95\% of pairs keep a correct slice under random product inputs (H3), it
loses at most five points of fault containment against the QDG slice (H4), and it costs less to inspect where both
contain the fault (H5). It starts from the QDG slice and checks every removal at $\ket{0\dots0}$, so H1 and the
directions of H2 and H5 hold by construction, and H3 and H4 are the real tests. We use exact McNemar tests on paired outcomes and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on paired
sizes, with Holm's correction across the five. Effect sizes come with bootstrap 95\% intervals and Wilson intervals
for proportions, and exploratory intervals resample whole algorithm families. Everything else is descriptive.
