Program slicing helps developers understand and debug code, and it has been proposed for quantum programs by reusing the classical slicing criterion and graph reachability over a quantum program dependence graph. No definition of quantum dependence has been checked against the semantics of quantum programs. We define it semantically, as the set of operations where a change can alter a qubit’s state for some input, and use this ground truth to evaluate dependence definitions on 784 circuit–qubit pairs from three benchmark suites, 119 circuits with known structure, and 5,688 seeded faults. Even the full classical program dependence graph, with data and control dependence, gives wrong slices for 26.0% of the pairs, because it misses the quantum dependence of a gate on its control qubit (phase kickback). Following entanglement, as the proposed quantum program dependence graph does, is sound but keeps 78.6% of the circuit. Adding quantum dependence, so that every multi-qubit gate reads and writes all its qubits, keeps 70.4%, is complete against the ground truth, and stays correct on every tested input, and its soundness is machine-checked. Commutation pruning shrinks these slices further and stays correct on all random inputs. State-based pruning shrinks them by 23.2%, but only for the input it was computed on. It removes the faulty operation in 769 of the seeded faults and lowers the fault-localization accuracy of three language models by up to 14.5 percentage points. A criterion over all affected qubits recovers 691 of these faults. Quantum dependence is not entanglement, and the results give tool builders a sound, input-independent dependence rule and a slicing criterion that fits quantum state.
