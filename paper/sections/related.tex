\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph*{Slicing and dependence}
Slicing was introduced by Weiser~\cite{weiser1982programmers} and reduced to reachability in the program
dependence graph by Ferrante et al.~\cite{ferrante1987pdg} and, across procedures, by Horwitz et
al.~\cite{horwitz1990interprocedural}. Dynamic slicing restricts a slice to one
execution~\cite{korel1988dynamic, agrawal1990dynamic}, much as state-based pruning restricts it to one
input, and surveys cover the many variants~\cite{tip1994a, harman2008program}. Probabilistic programs
already showed that classical dependence does not carry over unchanged, since observations add
dependences of their own~\cite{olmedo2025slicing}. For quantum programs, Yu and Zhao's quantum program
dependence graph~\cite{yu2025qpdg} adds entanglement, no-cloning, temporal and measurement dependences
to the classical graph and slices by reachability. It was not evaluated against the semantics of quantum
programs, and our study measures our reading of it beside the other rules. Static analysis frameworks for
quantum languages~\cite{behler2024supporting, behler2025static} provide the source representation that a
line-level slicer needs.

\paragraph*{Entanglement analysis}
A line of work approximates statically which qubits may be entangled, with type systems for patterns and
higher-order functions~\cite{perdrix2007types, prost2009reasoning}, abstract
interpretation~\cite{perdrix2008entanglement}, the stabilizer formalism~\cite{honda2015stabilizer},
a type system that tracks purity~\cite{yuan2022twist}, and dataflow rules for Q\#
programs~\cite{xia2025entanglement}. These analyses answer whether qubits are correlated, which matters
for optimization and for reasoning about measurement. They are not slicers, and Proposition~\ref{prop:ent}
shows that their answer does not decide dependence, since a qubit can depend on operations it is never
entangled with and need not depend on operations on its entangled partners.

\paragraph*{Light cones}
Restricting a circuit to the gates that can reach an observable is standard in simulation and
compilation, and Qiskit provides it as the \texttt{LightCone} transpiler pass~\cite{qiskit2025,
javadi2024qiskit}, which also removes gates that commute with the observable. We show that the plain
light cone is the sound and complete slice of a set of qubits, and that the commutation step is safe for
random inputs but not when the angles change.

\paragraph*{Debugging quantum programs}
Bug studies~\cite{zhao2021bugpatterns} and benchmarks such as Bugs4Q~\cite{zhao2023bugs4q} catalogue
real faults, static checkers such as QChecker~\cite{zhao2023qchecker} and LintQ~\cite{paltenghi2024lintq}
detect known bug patterns, and debugging tools let developers inspect circuits at chosen
points~\cite{metwalli2022debugging}. Mutation tools for quantum programs~\cite{mendiluze2021muskit,
fortunato2022qmutpy} and fault models~\cite{garcia2022testing} supply the faults of our study, and
mutation-based fault localization has been adapted to quantum programs by Ishimoto et
al.~\cite{ishimoto2025mbfl}. Slicing is complementary to these tools, since it tells them where to look.
