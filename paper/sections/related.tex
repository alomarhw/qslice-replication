\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph*{Program slicing}
Weiser introduced slicing~\cite{weiser1984program} and showed that programmers use slices when they
debug~\cite{weiser1982programmers}. Ottenstein and Ottenstein~\cite{ottenstein1984pdg}, Ferrante et
al.~\cite{ferrante1987pdg} and, across procedures, Horwitz et
al.~\cite{horwitz1990interprocedural} reduced it to reachability in a dependence graph, and dynamic
slicing restricts a slice to one execution~\cite{korel1988dynamic, agrawal1990dynamic}, much as state-based
pruning restricts it to one input. Surveys cover the many variants~\cite{tip1994a, harman2008program}.
General analysis frameworks such as WALA provide dependence-graph slicers for Java, JavaScript and, through
a separate front end, Python~\cite{wala}. Applied to a Qiskit program, such a slicer sees each gate as a
call on one circuit object, so it cannot tell which qubits an operation depends on. srcSlice computes
static slices from the srcML markup of classical programs~\cite{alomari2014srcslice}, as our tool does
for Qiskit programs, but it has no notion of qubits. A recent retrospective
notes that slices can be defined by almost any semantic method, as the application requires, and names
quantum programs as a future direction~\cite{gallagher2025program}. We follow that view and define the
slice of a quantum program by the reduced state of its criterion. Slicing of probabilistic programs
preserves the distribution of the criterion variables, and conditioning adds dependences of its
own~\cite{hur2014slicing, amtoft2020theory, olmedo2025slicing}. Our criterion is stronger. Preserving
$\rho_W$ preserves the outcome distribution of every measurement of $W$, so our slices are also
probabilistic slices of the measured program. The converse fails, because a distribution in one basis does
not see phase or the phase kickback that Proposition~\ref{prop:du} illustrates.

\paragraph*{Dependence in quantum programs}
To our knowledge, Yu and Zhao's QPDG~\cite{yu2025qpdg} is the only prior proposal of dependence-based
slicing for quantum programs. Its dependences are defined in prose. For example, an entanglement dependence
holds when the qubits of two statements are entangled, and the paper leaves an implementation and its
evaluation to future work. Its no-cloning example, \texttt{x(0)} followed by \texttt{cx(0,1)}, is described as
entangling the two qubits, although it produces the product state $\ket{11}$. The dependence is real but
the entanglement is not, which is case~(i) of Proposition~\ref{prop:ent}. We implement the graph as a
documented baseline and evaluate it against the semantics. Other program
representations model qubits without a dependence semantics. The quantum code property graph of Kaul et
al.~\cite{kaul2023uniform} adds a data-flow edge from the control of a CNOT to its target and treats the
control as unchanged. This is the classical reading that phase kickback breaks, as
Proposition~\ref{prop:du} shows. LintQ, a static analyzer for Qiskit programs~\cite{paltenghi2024lintq},
orders two operations exactly when they act on a common qubit, which is the relation of the QDG, and
uses it to find bug patterns, aiming by design at neither full soundness nor full precision. We show that
this relation gives sound and dependence-complete slices. Behler et al.\ extend srcML to OpenQASM and
analyze the order of the operations on each qubit~\cite{behler2024supporting}, and they name the slicing
of individual qubits as future work~\cite{behler2025static}. Our results give such a slicer its dependence
rule. Code-smell detectors also inspect the operations on each qubit, for example to flag idle
qubits~\cite{chen2023smelly}.

\paragraph*{Entanglement analysis}
A line of work approximates statically which qubits may be entangled, with type systems for patterns and
higher-order functions~\cite{perdrix2007types, prost2009reasoning}, abstract
interpretation~\cite{perdrix2008entanglement, yu2021abstract}, the stabilizer
formalism~\cite{honda2015stabilizer}, a type system that tracks purity~\cite{yuan2022twist}, and dataflow
rules for Q\# programs~\cite{xia2025entanglement}. The ScaffCC compiler conservatively assumes that two
qubits which interact may be entangled~\cite{javadiabhari2015scaffcc, zhao2023qchecker}, which is the
entanglement rule of our QPDG baseline. These analyses answer whether qubits are correlated, which matters for
optimization and for reasoning about measurement. They are not slicers, and Proposition~\ref{prop:ent}
shows that their answer does not decide dependence.

\paragraph*{Light cones and compilers}
Heim et al.\ observe that dependences between quantum instructions should account for instructions
that commute even when they act on the same qubits~\cite{heim2020quantum}. Qiskit's \texttt{LightCone}
pass restricts a circuit to the gates that can reach an observable and drops gates that commute with
it~\cite{qiskit2025}. Compilers build gate-dependence graphs to map and schedule circuits on
hardware~\cite{murali2019triq} and cancel or merge gates while keeping the circuit
equivalent~\cite{amy2020staq}. Light-cone arguments also bound which gates can affect an observable in the analysis of
QAOA~\cite{farhi2014qaoa}, and the frame rules of quantum separation logics reason locally about the qubits
a program touches~\cite{zhou2021qsl, le2022qsl}. None of them selects the operations relevant to a criterion
of a program, and Theorem~\ref{thm:sound} states the light-cone fact as a property of slices.

\paragraph*{Debugging quantum programs}
A recent roadmap finds that debugging research for quantum programs is still in an initial phase,
lists slicing among its opportunities, and finds almost no use of dependence graphs or data-flow
analysis~\cite{ramalho2025road}. The slicing it reviews is that of Metwalli and Van
Meter~\cite{metwalli2022debugging, metwalli2024testing}, which splits a circuit at breakpoints the
developer places and drops idle qubits. Qiskit's own slicing utilities likewise partition a circuit, by
barriers, depth, gate type or edge coloring~\cite{qiskitaddonutils}. Neither has a criterion, and the
developer still narrows the fault by hand. Metwalli and Van Meter's phase tests detect a wrong phase on a
slice, but locating the faulty qubit takes further test vectors that the developer designs by hand. A slice
for a joint criterion narrows where to look. Bug studies~\cite{zhao2021bugpatterns} and benchmarks such as
Bugs4Q~\cite{zhao2023bugs4q} catalog real faults, and static checkers such as
QChecker~\cite{zhao2023qchecker} and Q-PAC~\cite{nayak2023qpac} detect known bug and fix patterns. Mutation
tools~\cite{mendiluze2021muskit, fortunato2022qmutpy} and fault models~\cite{garcia2022testing} supply the
faults of our study, and mutation-based fault localization has been adapted to quantum programs by
Ishimoto et al.~\cite{ishimoto2025mbfl}. Slicing is complementary to these tools, since it restricts where
they look, although RQ3 shows that a restriction alone does not improve their normalized ranking.
