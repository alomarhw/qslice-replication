\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph*{Program slicing}
Weiser introduced slicing as a debugging aid~\cite{weiser1982programmers}. Ferrante et
al.~\cite{ferrante1987pdg} and, across procedures, Horwitz et al.~\cite{horwitz1990interprocedural}
reduced it to reachability in a dependence graph, and dynamic slicing restricts a slice to one
execution~\cite{korel1988dynamic, agrawal1990dynamic}, much as state-based pruning restricts it to one
input. Surveys cover the many variants~\cite{tip1994a, harman2008program}. A recent retrospective notes
that slices can be defined by almost any semantic method, as the application requires, and names quantum
programs as a future direction~\cite{gallagher2025program}. We follow that view and define the slice of a
quantum program by the reduced state of its criterion. Probabilistic programs already showed that
classical dependence does not carry over unchanged, since observations add dependences of their
own~\cite{olmedo2025slicing}.

\paragraph*{Dependence in quantum programs}
To our knowledge, Yu and Zhao's quantum program dependence graph~\cite{yu2025qpdg} is the only prior
proposal of dependence-based slicing for quantum programs. It adds entanglement, no-cloning, temporal and measurement
dependences to the classical graph, keeps the classical criterion $(s, V)$, and slices by reachability.
Its dependences are defined in prose, an entanglement dependence holding when the qubits of two
statements are entangled, and the paper leaves an implementation and its evaluation to future work. Its
no-cloning example, \texttt{x(0)} followed by \texttt{cx(0,1)}, is described as entangling the two qubits,
although it produces the product state $\ket{11}$. The dependence is real but the entanglement is not,
which is case~(i) of Proposition~\ref{prop:ent}. We operationalize the graph as the wire rule with
entanglement closure and evaluate it against the semantics. Other program representations model qubits
without a dependence semantics. The quantum code property graph of Kaul et al.~\cite{kaul2023uniform}
adds a data-flow edge from the control of a CNOT to its target and treats the control as unchanged. This
is the classical reading that phase kickback breaks, as Proposition~\ref{prop:du} shows. LintQ~\cite{paltenghi2024lintq}
orders two operations exactly when they act on a common qubit, which is the relation of the wire rule,
and uses it to find bug patterns while giving up soundness by design. We show that this relation is the
sound and complete basis for slicing. Behler et al.\ extend srcML to OpenQASM, analyze the order of the
operations on each qubit, and name the slicing of individual qubits as future
work~\cite{behler2024supporting, behler2025static}. Our results give such a slicer its dependence rule.
Studies of code smells take a similar per-qubit view of circuits~\cite{chen2023smelly}.

\paragraph*{Entanglement analysis}
A line of work approximates statically which qubits may be entangled, with type systems for patterns and
higher-order functions~\cite{perdrix2007types, prost2009reasoning}, abstract
interpretation~\cite{perdrix2008entanglement}, the stabilizer formalism~\cite{honda2015stabilizer}, a
type system that tracks purity~\cite{yuan2022twist}, and dataflow rules for Q\#
programs~\cite{xia2025entanglement}. These analyses answer whether qubits are correlated, which matters
for optimization and for reasoning about measurement. They are not slicers, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:ent} shows that their answer does not decide dependence.

\paragraph*{Light cones and compilers}
Heim et al.\ observe that dependences between quantum instructions should account for instructions
that commute even when they act on the same qubits~\cite{heim2020quantum}. Qiskit's \texttt{LightCone}
pass restricts a circuit to the gates that can reach an observable and drops gates that commute with
it~\cite{qiskit2025, javadi2024qiskit}. Compilers build gate-dependence graphs to map and schedule
circuits on hardware~\cite{murali2019triq} and cancel or merge gates while keeping the circuit
equivalent~\cite{amy2020staq}. None of them selects the operations relevant to a criterion. We show
that the plain light cone is the sound and complete slice of a set of qubits, and that commutation is
safe for random inputs but not when the angles change.

\paragraph*{Debugging quantum programs}
A recent roadmap finds that debugging research for quantum programs is still in an initial phase,
lists slicing among its opportunities, and finds almost no use of dependence graphs or data-flow
analysis~\cite{ramalho2025road}. The slicing it reviews is that of Metwalli and Van
Meter~\cite{metwalli2022debugging, metwalli2024testing}, which splits a circuit at breakpoints the
developer places and drops idle qubits. It has no criterion, and the developer still narrows the
fault by hand, since their phase tests show that a phase is wrong but not where to look. A criterion
over a set of qubits answers that question. Bug studies~\cite{zhao2021bugpatterns} and benchmarks such
as Bugs4Q~\cite{zhao2023bugs4q} catalogue real faults, and static checkers such as
QChecker~\cite{zhao2023qchecker} detect known bug patterns. Mutation
tools~\cite{mendiluze2021muskit, fortunato2022qmutpy} and fault models~\cite{garcia2022testing} supply
the faults of our study, and mutation-based fault localization has been adapted to quantum programs by
Ishimoto et al.~\cite{ishimoto2025mbfl}. Slicing is complementary to these tools, since it tells them
where to look.
