\section{Study Design}\label{sec:design}

We ask four research questions, each about a question a developer has of a slice, whether it can be
trusted, how small it can safely be, whether it holds the fault and what it costs.
\textbf{RQ1} asks which dependence rules give sound and dependence-complete slices.
\textbf{RQ2} asks how much smaller than the QDG slice a slice can safely be, and under which inputs the
extra precision holds.
\textbf{RQ3} asks what each rule does to fault localization, measured by whether the slice contains
the fault and by mutation-based fault localization.
\textbf{RQ4} asks what each rule costs in time, and, as an exploratory addition, how \tool{} scales on
LintQ's corpus, QASMBench's medium and large circuits and parametric programs of our own.

\paragraph*{Pre-registration}
The protocol was committed before the runs reported here. It extends an earlier run whose results it
reuses, so H1 and H2 below were known. Later changes are dated deviations, marked when made after a result.
The PDG and \texttt{LightCone} baselines came after the first runs. The dependence set as a size reference, the symbolic-angle variant, the
flow-sensitive and read-only QPDG, the static slicer, the measures of the foundations and the scenario of Section~\ref{sec:example} were
added after the results, are exploratory and enter none of the confirmatory tests. The five confirmatory
tests below concern state-based pruning. Every other result, including the classical PDG's share of wrong
slices, is exploratory or descriptive. The protocol also pre-registered two descriptive tasks for language
models, which we ran and report briefly in RQ3.

\paragraph*{Circuits}
Corpus~A has \numNCircuits{} circuits of at most \numMaxQubits{} qubits from MQT~Bench
(\numCircuitsMqt{})~\cite{quetschlich2023mqt}, QASMBench (\numCircuitsQasm{})~\cite{li2023qasmbench} and
VeriQBench (\numCircuitsVeriq{})~\cite{chen2022veriqbench}. Every qubit of every circuit is a criterion,
which gives \numNPairs{} circuit--qubit pairs, \numNDynamicPairs{} of them in dynamic circuits with
mid-circuit measurement, reset or conditioned gates. We also generate
\numFamCircuits{} circuits with known structure. Corpus~L has
\numCorpusLCircuits{} circuits of \numCorpusLMinQubits{} to \numCorpusLMaxQubits{} qubits.

\paragraph*{Programs for \tool{}}
\tool{} is evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:tool} and RQ4 on programs, not circuits. They are the Qiskit
programs of Bugs4Q, the \numToolTextbookQiskit{} notebooks of the Qiskit textbook~\cite{qiskittextbook}, the
\numScaleSOneFiles{} programs that LintQ's authors collected from GitHub~\cite{paltenghi2024lintq}, all read and
never executed, and QASMBench's medium and large circuits, of up to millions of operations. LintQ's public
warnings on its corpus, with the \numLqTp{} that its authors confirmed as bugs, are the data of the comparison
with LintQ.

\paragraph*{Oracle and ground truth}
An independent oracle simulates each circuit exactly, branching over measurement outcomes, and agrees with
Qiskit's statevector simulation to within \numOracleErr{}. A slice preserves the criterion when the squared Uhlmann fidelity between the
criterion's reduced states under the slice and under the whole circuit is at least $1 - 10^{-6}$. The
dependences of Definition~\ref{def:dep} are measured by inserting \numInfluencePerturbations{} random
unitaries after every operation and comparing the criterion's state at \numInfluenceInputs{} inputs,
$\ket{0\dots0}$ and random product states. A catalog of \numCatEntries{} hand-derived cases checks the
ground truth and every slicer before the runs.

\paragraph*{Slicers}
Each rule of Section~\ref{sec:dependence} is implemented on circuits from its read and write sets or its
pruning step. Exploratory variants make commutation pruning hold for all angles and give state-based
pruning a joint criterion.

\paragraph*{Robustness}
A slice computed at $\ket{0\dots0}$ is rechecked, with its operations unchanged, under \numRobDraws{} random
computational-basis inputs, \numRobDraws{} random product inputs and, for circuits with rotation angles,
\numRobDraws{} random rebindings of every angle. A pair is robust if the slice preserves the criterion in
every draw.

\paragraph*{Faults}
We seed faults with \numMutOperators{} operators, \numMutPublished{} from Muskit~\cite{mendiluze2021muskit},
QMutPy~\cite{fortunato2022qmutpy} and García de la Barrera et al.~\cite{garcia2022testing}, and an angle
perturbation of our own. Of the \numMutants{} mutants, \numMutantsExcluded{} with an empty fault site are
excluded, and \numMutantsKilled{} change the measured distribution of at least one qubit. Each such qubit
and its mutant form a unit, which gives \numRqThreeUnits{} units. For each we record the slice's size and whether it contains the fault site. Of the \numBugsFourQTriaged{} entries of Bugs4Q~\cite{zhao2023bugs4q},
\numBugsFourQIncluded{} are circuit-level faults that port mechanically to the current Qiskit release, and
\numBugsFourQKilled{} of them change a qubit's measured distribution. Their operations map to lines of the original programs, on which \tool{} computes the same line slices.

\paragraph*{Fault localization}
Mutation-based fault localization ranks the operations of the faulty circuit by how their mutants
change its observable behavior, with the Ochiai formula, as Ishimoto et al.\ do for quantum
programs~\cite{ishimoto2025mbfl}. It runs on a sample of
\numMbflUnits{} units, stratified by fault operator and including every Bugs4Q unit. We rank over the whole
circuit and within each slice and report the EXAM score, the share of ranked operations examined before
the fault, lower being better. Without fault localization, we also order each slice from its
last operation backward and score the fault's rank the same way. A slice never makes a developer examine
more operations, but EXAM divides by the number ranked, so a smaller slice can raise it.

\paragraph*{Statistics}
Five confirmatory hypotheses compare state-based pruning with the other rules. H1 states that it is wrong
on fewer pairs than def-use, H2 that its slices are smaller than QDG slices, and H3 that at least 95\% of
pairs keep a correct slice under random product inputs. H4 states that it loses at most five percentage
points of fault containment against the QDG slice, and H5 that its slices are smaller where both contain
the fault. State-based pruning starts from the QDG slice and checks every removal at $\ket{0\dots0}$, so H1
and the directions of H2 and H5 hold by construction, and H3 and H4 are the real tests. We use exact
McNemar tests on paired outcomes and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on paired sizes, with Holm's correction
across the five. Effect sizes come with bootstrap 95\% intervals, Wilson intervals for proportions, and exploratory intervals resample whole
algorithm families. Everything else is descriptive.
