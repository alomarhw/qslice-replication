\section{Study Design}\label{sec:design}

We ask four research questions.
\textbf{RQ1} asks which dependence rules give sound and dependence-complete slices.
\textbf{RQ2} asks how much smaller than the QDG slice a slice can safely be, and under which inputs the
extra precision holds.
\textbf{RQ3} asks what each rule does to fault localization, measured by whether the slice contains
the fault and by mutation-based fault localization.
\textbf{RQ4} asks what each rule costs in time.

\paragraph*{Pre-registration}
The protocol, including the slicers, metrics, tests, thresholds and the decision rules for the
paper's framing, was committed before the runs reported here. It extends the protocol of an earlier run of
this study, whose results it reuses, so H1 and H2 below were known when it was written. Every later change
is recorded as a dated deviation with its reason, and those made after a result are marked as such. The QDG
is the protocol's light-cone baseline. State-based pruning turned out sound but failed the robustness
condition, and the paper's framing follows the protocol's decision rule for that case. The classical
PDG and an order-preserving mapping for Qiskit's \texttt{LightCone} pass were added as baselines after the
first runs. The dependence set as a reference for slice size, the symbolic-angle variant of commutation
pruning, the flow-sensitive QPDG, the read-only reading of the QPDG, the static slicer and its forward slices and profiles, the measures of
the foundations in Section~\ref{sec:results}, and the seeded scenario of Section~\ref{sec:example} were all
added after the results, and all are exploratory. The acceptance tests of the slicer were fixed before any
of its output. None of these changes enters the five confirmatory tests, and no unit test in the code asserts an
expected study outcome.

\paragraph*{Circuits}
Corpus~A has \numNCircuits{} circuits of at most \numMaxQubits{} qubits from three benchmark suites,
MQT~Bench (\numCircuitsMqt{} circuits)~\cite{quetschlich2023mqt}, QASMBench
(\numCircuitsQasm{})~\cite{li2023qasmbench} and VeriQBench (\numCircuitsVeriq{})~\cite{chen2022veriqbench}.
Every qubit of every circuit is a criterion, which gives \numNPairs{} circuit--qubit pairs. Of these,
\numNDynamicPairs{} are in dynamic circuits, which measure or reset a qubit before the end or condition
gates on measured bits. We also generate \numFamCircuits{} circuits with known structure. They cover depth
sweeps at fixed width, multipartite states that are partly uncomputed by inverse gates, chains that
entangle, disentangle and re-entangle, indirect entanglement through a third qubit, and inverse pairs
$U \dots U^{\dagger}$. For scale, corpus~L has \numCorpusLCircuits{} larger circuits of \numCorpusLMinQubits{} to \numCorpusLMaxQubits{} qubits.

\paragraph*{Oracle and ground truth}
All slices are judged by one independent oracle. It simulates the circuit's unconditional state exactly,
by branching over measurement outcomes, and shares no code with any slicer. It agrees with Qiskit's
statevector simulation to within \numOracleErr{} on random circuits. A slice preserves the criterion when
the squared Uhlmann fidelity, the standard similarity measure for quantum states, between the criterion's
reduced states under the slice and under the whole circuit is at least $1 - 10^{-6}$. The dependences of
Definition~\ref{def:dep} are measured by inserting \numInfluencePerturbations{} random unitaries after
every operation and comparing the criterion's state at \numInfluenceInputs{} inputs, $\ket{0\dots0}$ and
ten random product states, in which each qubit has its own random state and none is entangled. A
dependence catalog of \numCatEntries{} hand-derived cases, including the examples of
Section~\ref{sec:dependence}, checks the ground truth and every slicer before the runs.

\paragraph*{Slicers}
Each rule of Section~\ref{sec:dependence} is implemented on circuits, directly from its read and write
sets or its pruning step. An exploratory variant of commutation pruning replaces every angle by a symbol
before Qiskit's pass, so that commutation must hold for all angles, and a variant of state-based pruning
takes a joint criterion.

\paragraph*{Robustness}
A slice computed at $\ket{0\dots0}$ is rechecked, with its operations unchanged, under \numRobDraws{} random
computational-basis inputs, that is, random bit strings, \numRobDraws{} random product inputs and, for
circuits with rotation angles, \numRobDraws{} random rebindings of every angle. A pair is robust if the
slice preserves the criterion in every draw.

\paragraph*{Faults}
We seed faults with \numMutOperators{} operators, \numMutPublished{} taken from Muskit~\cite{mendiluze2021muskit},
QMutPy~\cite{fortunato2022qmutpy} and the fault model of García de la Barrera et
al.~\cite{garcia2022testing}, and an angle perturbation of our own. They insert, delete and replace gates,
insert and delete mid-circuit measurements, swap a control with a target, remove a control, re-target an
operand, corrupt the preparation of an entangling gate, and perturb an angle. Of the \numMutants{}
mutants, \numMutantsExcluded{} with an empty fault site are excluded, and \numMutantsKilled{} change the
measured distribution of at least one qubit. Each such qubit forms a unit with its mutant, which gives
\numRqThreeUnits{} units, each with a criterion qubit whose measured distribution is wrong. For each unit we
record whether the slice of the faulty circuit contains the fault site, which is the mutated operation or,
for a deletion, the operations next to the deletion point, and how many operations the slice keeps. The
real bugs come from Bugs4Q~\cite{zhao2023bugs4q}. Of \numBugsFourQTriaged{} entries,
\numBugsFourQIncluded{} are circuit-level faults whose buggy and fixed programs can be ported mechanically
to the current Qiskit release. Of these, \numBugsFourQKilled{} change a qubit's measured distribution,
\numBugsFourQInjured{} changes the state of a qubit but no measured distribution, and \numBugsFourQAlive{} changes no qubit. The remaining
\numBugsFourQExcluded{} are excluded, one because its buggy and fixed programs differ in qubit count and
one because it uses an initialization the oracle does not support. Each operation of the ported circuits
is mapped to its line in the original program, and Section~\ref{sec:tool} shows that our tool computes the
same line slices from the original programs.

\paragraph*{Fault localization}
Mutation-based fault localization mutates the operations of the faulty circuit and ranks them by how their
mutants change its observable behavior, scored with the Ochiai suspiciousness formula, as Ishimoto et al.\
do for quantum programs~\cite{ishimoto2025mbfl}. Because it simulates many mutants per unit, it runs on a sample of \numMbflUnits{} units, stratified by
fault operator and including every Bugs4Q unit. We rank over the whole circuit and within each slice and
report the EXAM score, the share of the ranked operations a developer examines before reaching the fault,
where lower is better. Without fault localization, we also order each slice from its last operation
backward and score the fault's rank the same way. Because a slice ranks a subset of the operations in the same order, the developer never examines
more operations within a slice than in the whole circuit, but EXAM divides by the number ranked, so a
smaller slice can raise it.

\paragraph*{Statistics}
Five confirmatory hypotheses compare state-based pruning with the other rules. H1 states that it is wrong
on fewer pairs than def-use, H2 that its slices are smaller than QDG slices, and H3 that at least 95\% of
pairs keep a correct slice under random product inputs. H4 states that it loses at most five percentage
points of fault containment against the QDG slice, and H5 that its slices are smaller where both contain
the fault. State-based pruning starts from the QDG slice and checks every removal at $\ket{0\dots0}$, the
input at which RQ1 judges it, so H1 and the directions of H2 and H5 hold by construction and only their
effect sizes are informative. H3 and H4 are the real tests. We test them with exact McNemar tests, which compare
paired yes-or-no outcomes, and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on paired sizes, and Holm's method corrects across
the five. Effect sizes come first,
with bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals (10{,}000 resamples) and Wilson intervals for proportions. Since
pairs and units cluster in circuits and algorithm families, we also report exploratory intervals that
resample whole families.
Everything else is descriptive.
