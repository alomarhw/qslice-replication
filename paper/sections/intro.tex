\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Program slicing finds the statements of a program that can affect a value at a point of
interest~\cite{weiser1982programmers}. A slice narrows the code a developer must read to understand that
value, and developers use slices when they debug. Quantum programs are hard to understand and debug. A
quantum program builds a circuit, a sequence of gates that act on qubits, the quantum counterpart of
bits, and measurements that read qubits into classical bits. Its intermediate states cannot be inspected
without disturbing them, so assertions on a state cost extra measurements, extra qubits or many runs~\cite{svore2018qsharp}. Its
outcomes are probabilistic, and the effect of a gate on one qubit may show only in the outcomes of
another~\cite{ali2022sequantum, murillo2025roadmap}. Understanding quantum code also requires knowledge of
physics and mathematics that many software developers lack~\cite{daloisio2024llm}. Static checkers find
known bug patterns, but 22 of the 42 bugs of the Bugs4Q benchmark on which QChecker was evaluated are
wrong outputs that it cannot detect~\cite{zhao2023qchecker}. A slicer that tells a developer which lines
of a quantum program can influence a wrong outcome would help them understand the code and find such bugs.

Slicing has been proposed for quantum programs. Yu and Zhao's quantum program dependence graph
(QPDG)~\cite{yu2025qpdg} extends the classical program dependence graph (PDG)~\cite{ferrante1987pdg} with
entanglement, no-cloning, temporal and measurement dependences. The QPDG reduces slicing to graph reachability
under the classical slicing criterion $(s, V)$, which names a statement $s$ and the variables $V$ whose
values the slice must preserve. A roadmap of quantum software engineering describes this graph as
facilitating slicing and dependence analysis for debugging, testing and
understanding~\cite{murillo2025roadmap}, and a later roadmap notes that validating such models remains
challenging~\cite{ye2026llmqse}. No definition of quantum dependence has been checked against the
semantics of quantum programs. It is therefore not known whether the slices such definitions produce
contain the operations a developer must inspect.

Section~\ref{sec:example} shows where this matters, on the Bernstein--Vazirani program of the Qiskit
textbook with a seeded fault. Reading a two-qubit gate as a classical assignment gives a slice that omits
the faulty line and still reproduces the qubit's state. A real bug from Bugs4Q adds that a fault may leave
the measured outcomes of each qubit unchanged, so a slice must be judged by states rather than by
histograms.

This paper defines quantum dependence semantically, as the set of operations where a change can alter
the state of the criterion qubits for some input, and generalizes the criterion to a set of qubits whose
joint state before readout the slice must preserve. A slice is sound if running it gives the criterion
qubits the same state as the whole program for every input, and dependence-complete if it contains every
dependence. We measure how dependence definitions behave against this ground truth, in a pre-registered
study whose later changes are logged as dated deviations, on \numNPairs{} circuit--qubit pairs from three benchmark suites, \numFamCircuits{}
circuits with known structure, \numRqThreeUnits{} fault--qubit units from \numMutantsKilled{} seeded
faults, and real bugs from Bugs4Q~\cite{zhao2023bugs4q}. The definitions range from the classical PDG to
state-based pruning, which removes operations by simulating the circuit at one input, and they include a
documented reimplementation of the QPDG as a baseline.

A correct slice needs data, control and quantum dependence. The classical PDG has the first two and still
gives wrong slices for \numCpdgUnsoundPct{} of the pairs, because a two-qubit gate can change its control
qubit, for instance by phase kickback. Our reimplementation of the QPDG is sound by construction but keeps
\numRqOneYzSize{} of the circuit, and under the reading in which a gate writes only its targets it is wrong
on \numYzRoUnsound{} pairs. The quantum dependence graph (QDG), in which every gate reads and writes all its
qubits, is the backward light cone that LintQ and Qiskit's \texttt{LightCone} pass already compute. Its
soundness follows from the locality of quantum operations, and what we add is the semantics that states it
and the measurement of what it costs. It keeps \numRqOneLcSize{}, only \numLcExcess{} percentage points
above the dependences themselves. Entanglement turns out to be neither necessary nor sufficient for
dependence. State-based pruning shrinks QDG slices by \numHTwoRelative{}, but only for the input it was
computed on, and it removes the faulty operation in \numCQFour{} units.

This paper gives quantum program slicing a semantic basis. Section~\ref{sec:dependence} defines quantum
dependence and slice correctness semantically and proves that the QDG gives sound and
dependence-complete slices. Section~\ref{sec:tool}
presents a static slicer for Qiskit programs~\cite{javadi2024qiskit}, Section~\ref{sec:results} gives the
first comparison of quantum dependence definitions against a semantic ground truth, and
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} lays out a roadmap of open problems. A replication package regenerates every
number in the paper.
