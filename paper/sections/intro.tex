\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Program slicing finds the statements of a program that can affect a value at a point of
interest~\cite{weiser1982programmers}. A slice narrows the code a developer must read to understand that
value, and developers use slices when they debug. Quantum programs are hard to understand and debug. A
quantum program builds a circuit, a sequence of gates that act on qubits, the quantum counterpart of
bits, and measurements that read qubits into classical bits. Its intermediate states cannot be inspected
without disturbing them, so assertions on a state work only in simulation~\cite{svore2018qsharp}. Its
outcomes are probabilistic, and the effect of a gate on one qubit may show only in the outcomes of
another~\cite{ali2022sequantum, murillo2025roadmap}. Understanding quantum code also requires knowledge of
physics and mathematics that many software developers lack~\cite{daloisio2024llm}. Static checkers find
known bug patterns, but 22 of the 42 bugs of the Bugs4Q benchmark on which QChecker was evaluated are
wrong outputs that it cannot detect~\cite{zhao2023qchecker}. A slicer that tells a developer which lines
of a quantum program can influence a wrong outcome would help them understand the code and find such bugs.

Slicing has been proposed for quantum programs. Yu and Zhao's quantum program dependence graph
(QPDG)~\cite{yu2025qpdg} extends the classical program dependence graph (PDG)~\cite{ferrante1987pdg} with
entanglement, no-cloning, temporal and measurement dependences. Two qubits are entangled when their joint
state cannot be described as a state of each qubit alone. The QPDG reduces slicing to graph reachability
under the classical slicing criterion $(s, V)$, which names a statement $s$ and the variables $V$ whose
values the slice must preserve. A roadmap of quantum software engineering describes this graph as
facilitating slicing and dependence analysis for debugging, testing and
understanding~\cite{murillo2025roadmap}, and a later roadmap notes that validating such models remains
challenging~\cite{ye2026llmqse}. No definition of quantum dependence has been checked against the
semantics of quantum programs. It is therefore not known whether the slices such definitions produce
contain the operations a developer must inspect.

Section~\ref{sec:example} shows a real bug where this matters. Reading a two-qubit gate as a classical
assignment gives a slice that omits the faulty line and still reproduces the qubit's state, and the bug
itself shows only in the correlation between two qubits, which a single-variable criterion cannot
express.

This paper defines quantum dependence semantically, as the set of operations where a change can alter
the state of the criterion qubits for some input, and generalizes the criterion to a set of qubits whose
joint state the slice must preserve. A slice is sound if running it gives the criterion qubits the same
state as the whole program for every input, and dependence-complete if it contains every dependence. We
measure how dependence definitions behave against this ground truth, in a study whose protocol we fixed
before any run, on \numNPairs{} circuit--qubit pairs from three benchmark suites, \numFamCircuits{}
circuits with known structure, \numRqThreeUnits{} fault--qubit units from \numMutantsKilled{} seeded
faults, and real bugs from Bugs4Q~\cite{zhao2023bugs4q}. The definitions range from the classical PDG to
state-based pruning, which removes operations by simulating the circuit at one input, and they include our
documented reading of the QPDG.

A correct slice needs data, control and quantum dependence. The classical PDG has the first two and still
gives wrong slices for \numCpdgUnsoundPct{} of the pairs. A two-qubit gate can change its control qubit,
an effect called phase kickback, and no classical reading of the gate expresses this quantum dependence.
Following entanglement, as the QPDG does, is sound but keeps \numRqOneYzSize{} of the circuit. The wire
rule, in which every gate reads and writes all its qubits, keeps \numRqOneLcSize{}, only \numLcExcess{}
percentage points above the dependences themselves. It misses no dependence and stays correct on every
tested input, and its soundness proof is mechanized for an abstract model of local operations.
Entanglement turns out to be neither necessary nor sufficient for dependence. Commutation pruning shrinks
the wire slices further, and it stays correct when angles change only if it treats them as symbols.
State-based pruning shrinks them by \numHTwoRelative{}, but only for the input it was computed on. It
removes the faulty operation in \numCQFour{} units and lowers the fault-localization accuracy of three of
the four language models we tested by up to \numTTwoMaxDrop{} percentage points. A joint criterion over
all wrong qubits recovers \numCQFourRecovered{} of these units.

This paper makes five contributions. Section~\ref{sec:dependence} gives semantic definitions of quantum
dependence, slice soundness and dependence completeness, with a criterion over sets of qubits, and proves
that the wire rule is sound and dependence-complete. Section~\ref{sec:tool} presents a tool that slices
programs written with Qiskit, IBM's open-source Python framework for quantum
programs~\cite{javadi2024qiskit}, from their source and without running them, and reports the lines and
loop iterations of a slice. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports, to our knowledge, the first comparison of
quantum dependence definitions against a semantic ground truth, covering soundness, completeness,
robustness to inputs and precision. It also measures what each definition does to fault localization on
seeded faults and real bugs, by whether the slice contains the fault, with mutation-based fault
localization and with language models. Finally, a replication package regenerates every number in the
paper. Section~\ref{sec:design} describes the study design, and Sections~\ref{sec:discussion}
to~\ref{sec:conclusion} discuss the results, the threats to validity and related work, and conclude.
