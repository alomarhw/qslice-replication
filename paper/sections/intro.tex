\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A developer who sees a qubit end in the wrong state asks which lines of the program determine it. For
classical programs, program slicing answers it. A slice keeps the statements that can affect a value of
interest, narrows the code a developer must read, and developers use slices when they
debug~\cite{weiser1982programmers}. Quantum programs are harder to comprehend. A quantum program builds a
circuit of gates and measurements on qubits, its intermediate states cannot be inspected without disturbing
them, and the effect of a gate on one qubit may show only in the outcomes of
another~\cite{svore2018qsharp, ali2022sequantum, murillo2025roadmap}, so understanding it requires physics
that many developers lack~\cite{daloisio2024llm}. Static checkers find known bug
patterns, but 22 of the 42 Bugs4Q bugs on which QChecker was evaluated are wrong outputs that it cannot
detect~\cite{zhao2023qchecker}. For such faults, a slice that tells the developer which lines can influence
the wrong outcome is the natural aid.

Slicing has been proposed for quantum programs. Yu and Zhao's quantum program dependence graph
(QPDG)~\cite{yu2025qpdg} extends the classical program dependence graph (PDG)~\cite{ferrante1987pdg} with
entanglement, no-cloning, temporal and measurement dependences and keeps the classical criterion. Roadmaps
describe this graph as an aid to understanding~\cite{murillo2025roadmap} whose validation remains
open~\cite{ye2026llmqse}. No definition of quantum dependence has been checked against what quantum
programs compute, so a developer cannot know whether a slice shows the code that matters. Worse, a slice
can look right and hide the fault. Section~\ref{sec:example} shows a classical reading of a
two-qubit gate giving a slice that omits the faulty line and still reproduces the qubit's state.

This paper asks what a quantum slice should look like to support comprehension, on three levels. What a
slice must keep is defined semantically. An operation is a dependence when changing it can alter the joint
state of the criterion qubits for some input, and a slice is sound if it gives these qubits the same state
as the whole program for every input. Which rules produce such slices is measured in a pre-registered study
on \numNPairs{} circuit--qubit pairs, \numFamCircuits{} circuits with known structure, \numRqThreeUnits{}
fault--qubit units and real bugs from Bugs4Q~\cite{zhao2023bugs4q}, from the classical PDG to state-based
pruning, which simulates one input, with a documented reimplementation of the QPDG. How a slice is shown to
a developer is answered by a static slicer for Qiskit programs~\cite{javadi2024qiskit}, built on srcML, that
reports source lines, loop iterations, the classical lines that compute them and a profile of every qubit.

The classical PDG gives wrong slices for \numCpdgUnsoundPct{} of the pairs, because a two-qubit gate can
change its control qubit, as in phase kickback, which no classical reading of the gate expresses. Of these
wrong slices, \numFdCpdgZMatchPct{} reproduce the measured outcomes, so a developer checking a slice by its
histogram would accept them. Entanglement turns out to be neither necessary nor sufficient for dependence,
and the QPDG's entanglement edges make it keep \numRqOneYzSize{} of a circuit. The quantum dependence graph
(QDG), in which every gate reads and writes all its qubits, keeps \numRqOneLcSize{}, only \numLcExcess{}
points above the dependences themselves, and is sound for every input. It is the known backward light cone, so what we add
is the semantics that justifies it as a slice and the measurement of what the alternatives cost. State-based pruning is \numHTwoRelative{} smaller,
but its slices hold for one input and lose a fault that the QDG slice keeps in \numCQFour{} units. Slices
narrow where a developer looks but do not improve mutation-based fault ranking, a gap that a study with
developers must close.

Section~\ref{sec:discussion} draws the implications for comprehension tools and the open problems. A
