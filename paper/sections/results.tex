\subsection{RQ1: Which rules give sound and complete slices?}

Table~\ref{tab:rq1} shows that only the rules with quantum dependence are sound. The classical PDG gives a
wrong slice for \numCpdgUnsound{} of the \numNPairs{} pairs, or \numCpdgUnsoundPct{}, with a 95\% interval of
\numCcCpdgUnsound{} when the \numCcFamilies{} algorithm families are resampled as clusters. Its control
dependence matters only on dynamic circuits, where it is wrong on \numCpdgDynamicUnsound{} pair against
\numDuDynamicUnsound{} for def-use. Of its \numCpdgMissTotal{} missed dependences, \numCpdgMissSameWire{} are controlled gates whose control
is the criterion qubit, \numCpdgMissKickback{} reach the criterion only through such a gate,
\numCpdgMissWritten{} reach it through a control further along the chain, and only \numCpdgMissFeedback{}
pass through classical feedback. Every miss but these \numCpdgMissFeedback{} follows from reading a control
as unchanged.
Many of these wrong slices look right. Of the \numCpdgUnsound{}, \numFdCpdgZMatch{} give the criterion the
same distribution of outcomes in the computational basis as the whole circuit, so only
\numFdCpdgObservable{} pairs, or \numFdCpdgObservablePct{}, are wrong in what a terminal measurement reads.
The others are wrong in phase, which a histogram check would accept but which changes the outcome once the
slice is composed with later gates or measured in another basis. As H1 expects by construction, state-based
pruning, which starts from the QDG slice, is wrong on fewer pairs than def-use, \numRqOneQUnsound{} against
\numRqOneDuUnsound{} with exact McNemar and Holm \numHOnePHolm{}.

The QDG is sound on every pair and recovers every measured dependence, as Theorem~\ref{thm:sound} and
Proposition~\ref{prop:complete} predict. Our reimplementation of the QPDG is sound by construction, since its
data edges let every gate write all its qubits, but its entanglement edges bring in only operations that are
not dependences, so its precision falls from \numRqOneLcPrecision{} to \numRqOneYzPrecision{} and its slices
grow from \numRqOneLcSize{} to \numRqOneYzSize{} of the circuit. The flow-sensitive QPDG still keeps
\numYzfExcess{} percentage points above the dependence set, so the imprecision comes from the entanglement
edges and not from our reading of them. Under the other reading of Yu and Zhao's data dependence, in which a
controlled gate writes only its targets, the QPDG is wrong on \numYzRoUnsound{} pairs from
\numYzRoUnsoundCircuits{} circuits with either entanglement rule. Its entanglement edges recover most of
what read-only controls lose, but it still misses \numYzRoMissed{} dependences, \numYzRoMissedKick{} of
them by kickback. The QDG and the QPDG are also sound on all \numCorpusLSoundPairs{} pairs of the
\numCorpusLSoundCircuits{} corpus-L circuits that the simulator can check exactly, where def-use is wrong
on \numCorpusLWrongDu{}.

Exploratory measures support Section~\ref{sec:dependence}. A graph with the typed edges of the QPDG and one entanglement edge per two-qubit gate gives exactly the QDG
slice on all \numFdFourEdgeNoClosureEqual{} pairs and differs only when these edges are closed
transitively, so the per-gate edge is the quantum dependence of the QDG and the closure is what costs
precision. Undirected slices keep \numFdUndSize{} of the circuit, and forward slices reach every qubit an operation
influences, as Proposition~\ref{prop:dual} predicts.

Slice sizes should be read against the dependence set, which makes up \numDepFloor{} of a circuit on
average. The QDG slice keeps only \numLcExcess{} percentage points more and equals it exactly on
\numLcExact{} of the \numNPairs{} pairs, while the QPDG keeps \numYzExcess{} points more. Slices shrink as circuits widen and less of a circuit can reach one qubit. At \numWElevenLo{} to \numWElevenHi{} qubits the QDG slice keeps \numWElevenLc{} of the
circuit and on corpus~L \numCorpusLSizeLc{}, while the QPDG stays near \numCorpusLSizeYz{}, because it links
the operations of a whole coupling component. Commutation pruning falls below the dependence set, to
\numWElevenLcq{} at the same widths, because it is not dependence-complete.

\subsection{RQ2: How much precision beyond the QDG slice is safe?}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_robust.pdf}
  \caption{Share of pairs whose slice, computed at $\ket{0\dots0}$, stays correct in all \numRobDraws{}
  draws of each input regime, for RQ2. Commutation is shown on the \numLcqApplicable{} pairs where Qiskit's
  pass applies, state-based pruning on the \numHThreePrunedN{} pairs it prunes, and angle rebinding on the
  pairs of circuits with rotation angles.}
  \label{fig:robust}
\end{figure}

Both refinements make slices smaller, but only commutation pruning keeps them correct for fixed angles. State-based pruning
shrinks the QDG slice by \numHTwoDiff{} percentage points of the circuit (95\% CI \numHTwoCILow{} to
\numHTwoCIHigh{}) and is smaller on \numHTwoSmaller{} pairs
(H2, Wilcoxon, Holm \numHTwoPHolm{}). Fig.~\ref{fig:robust} shows what that costs. Of the pairs it
prunes, the slice stays correct for all random product inputs on only \numHThreePrunedRate{}, and over
all pairs on \numHThreeRate{} (Wilson CI \numHThreeCILow{} to \numHThreeCIHigh{}, and \numCcHThree{}
with families as clusters), far below the pre-registered threshold of 95\%, so H3 fails. A state-based slice is therefore a property of one input,
not of the program.

Commutation pruning is smaller than the QDG slice on \numLcqSmallerThanLc{} of \numLcqApplicable{} pairs,
stays correct on every random basis and product input, and fails only when the angles change, on
\numRobLcqParamFail{} of \numRobLcqParamN{} pairs of variational circuits. Its symbolic variant, at a mean
size of \numLcqsSize{} against \numLcqSize{}, stays correct on every pair in every regime. Its recall of
\numLcqRecall{} shows that it misses dependences, since a different gate in place of one it drops could
change the criterion.

\paragraph*{Noise}
In a pre-registered descriptive analysis under depolarizing noise at two-qubit error rates of $10^{-3}$ to
$3\cdot10^{-2}$, the criterion's noisy states under the QDG slice and the whole circuit differ in trace
distance by at most \numNoiseLcCrossExactMax{} where the simulation is exact and \numNoiseLcCrossShotMax{}
where it samples, which is rounding and sampling error, as Theorem~\ref{thm:sound} predicts for noise local
to a gate. The state-based slice differs in \numNoiseQCrossExactNonzero{} of \numNoiseQCrossExactN{} exact
runs, by up to \numNoiseQCrossExactMax{}, because it removes noisy gates that the whole program
executes~\cite{preskill2018nisq}, so it can hide the noise a developer is trying to diagnose.

\subsection{RQ3: What does each rule do to fault localization?}

The QDG slice of the faulty circuit contains the fault site in \numRqThreeContainLc{} of the
\numRqThreeUnits{} units, against \numRqThreeContainYz{} for the QPDG, \numRqThreeContainCpdg{} for the
classical PDG, \numRqThreeContainDu{} for def-use, \numRqThreeContainQ{} for state-based pruning, and
\numRqThreeContainRandom{} for a random set of the state-based slice's size. Containment grows with size, and
the QDG and QPDG slices keep \numRqThreeSizeLc{} and \numRqThreeSizeYz{} of the faulty circuits. Against
a random set of its own size, every rule contains the fault more often, the QDG by \numSizeCtlLcGain{} points
(family CI \numSizeCtlLcGainCi{}), so containment is not only a matter of size. The QPDG's lead over the QDG
comes from size, since a random set of its size already holds the fault in \numSizeCtlYzRandom{} of the units. All
\numLcMissTotal{} units the QDG slice misses are omission faults, a removed control (\numLcMissCrm{}), a
re-targeted operand (\numLcMissQsw{}), a removed preparation of an entangling gate (\numLcMissEnt{}) or a
deleted gate (\numLcMissDel{}). Each deletes the interaction that the QDG slice of the faulty program would
follow. The QPDG contains
\numLcMissYzContained{} of them, because its closure still links operations that the faulty program no
longer connects.

State-based pruning lowers containment by \numHFourDiffAbs{} percentage points against the QDG slice
(95\% CI \numHFourCILow{} to \numHFourCIHigh{}, and \numCcHFour{} with families as clusters), so H4,
which allowed a loss of at most five points, fails.
In \numCQFour{} units the QDG slice contains the fault and the state-based slice does not. Pruning removes
an operation when the criterion is uncorrelated with its qubits at the input, so a fault that breaks a
correlation can make the faulty operation look irrelevant to the single criterion qubit. A joint criterion
over all wrong qubits recovers \numCQFourRecovered{} of these units. Where both slices contain the fault,
the state-based slice is smaller by \numHFiveSizeDiff{} operations on average, so H5 holds, but its EXAM
score is higher by \numHFiveExamQHigher{}. With mutation-based fault localization on the \numMbflPairedN{}
of \numMbflUnits{} units where both contain the fault, the mean EXAM score is \numMbflPairedAll{}
over the whole circuit, \numMbflPairedLc{} in the QDG slice and \numMbflPairedQ{} in the state-based
slice, so neither slice improves the normalized ranking. On the \numMbflAllN{} units whose fault every rule's slice contains, the ranking reaches the fault
after \numMbflAllExaminedMin{} to \numMbflAllExaminedMax{} operations on average, whichever slice it ranks,
so no rule saves more than a fraction of an operation.

On the \numLinesUnits{} real Bugs4Q units whose faulty lines map to the program, the QDG slices keep every
faulty line and \numLinesLcMean{} of \numLinesFullMean{} lines on average, so on these small programs
slicing barely reduces what there is to read. Def-use, which equals the
classical PDG on these unitary programs, loses the faulty line in \numLinesDuMissed{} of these units and,
for the bug of Section~\ref{sec:example}, which changes no measured distribution and is not among them,
omits the faulty \texttt{h(1)}.

\subsection{RQ4: What does it cost?}

The QDG slice costs at most \numCostLcMaxCritMs{}~ms per criterion at every depth. State-based pruning,
which simulates the circuit for every candidate removal, exceeds the per-circuit limit of
\numCostWallLimit{}~s for \numCostDepthEightyOverLimit{} of the \numCostDepthCircuits{} circuits at the
second-largest depth and for all \numCostDepthOneSixtyOverLimit{} at the largest, grows exponentially with
width, and completes on \numCorpusLCompleted{} of the \numCorpusLCircuits{} circuits of corpus~L. Building
the QPDG takes \numCostBuildDepthForty{}~s at depth \numCostMeasuredDepthForty{} and
\numCostBuildDepthOneSixty{}~s at depth \numCostMeasuredDepthOneSixty{} in the median, against
milliseconds for the QDG slice, because its closure links every pair of operations on a coupling component
while the QDG needs only the edges of each qubit.

\paragraph*{Scalability of \tool{}}
On the \numScaleSOneFiles{} programs of LintQ's corpus, read without being run, \tool{} slices the
\numScaleSOneSliced{} that build a circuit, every qubit up to 64 of them, in \numScaleSOneMedianMs{}~ms per program
in the median and within \numScaleSOneNinetyNine{}~s for 99\% of them, and \numScaleSOneTimeouts{} programs reach
the limit of 120~s. srcML and srcSlice take most of the median, \numScaleSOneSrcmlMs{} and
\numScaleSOneSrcsliceMs{}~ms, and the slices themselves \numScaleSOneSliceMs{}~ms. The largest trace, of
\numScaleSOneMaxOps{} operations, takes \numScaleSOneMaxOpsS{}~s. Fig.~\ref{fig:scale} shows the time against the
size. On the \numScaleQbRead{} circuits of QASMBench's medium and large sets that are valid OpenQASM~2, of up to
\numScaleQbMaxOps{} operations and \numScaleQbMaxQubits{} qubits, the one-pass slice of every qubit takes at most
\numScaleQbAllS{}~s, on the largest circuit. Qiskit's DAG slices one of its \numScaleQbMaxOpsQubits{} qubits in
\numScaleQbDagOneS{}~s after \numScaleQbDagLoadS{}~s of loading, which is about \numScaleQbDagAllMin{} minutes for
all of them. Reading the file is \tool{}'s larger cost there, \numScaleQbReadS{}~s with a reader written in Python.
The QPDG reimplementation builds its graph within the 600~s limit for circuits of up to \numScaleQpdgMaxOps{}
operations, in \numScaleQpdgMaxS{}~s, where \tool{} reads and slices the circuit in \numScaleQpdgSqS{}~s. It reaches the
limit on the next circuits by size, and the \numScaleQpdgOver{} larger ones are beyond it. Qiskit's \texttt{LightCone} pass, which also prunes commuting gates, slices one qubit of the circuit of
\numScaleLcMaxOps{} operations in \numScaleLcMaxS{}~s, where \tool{} slices all \numScaleLcQubits{} of its
qubits in \numScaleLcSqS{}~s, and exceeds the limit on \numScaleLcOver{} circuits. On our parametric programs the source stays at
about a dozen lines while the trace grows. The QFT on 600 qubits unrolls \numScaleQftOps{} operations from
\numScaleQftLines{} code lines in \numScaleQftS{}~s, where running the program with Qiskit and slicing one qubit
takes \numScaleQftRunS{}~s, which a static slicer gives up in order never to execute the program. Two of these programs loop beyond the interpreter's limit of 10{,}000 iterations, and \tool{} reads those loops
as loops of unknown length, as it does a loop whose bound it cannot compute.
