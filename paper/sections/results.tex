\subsection{RQ1: Which definitions give sound and complete slices?}

Table~\ref{tab:rq1} shows that only the rules with quantum dependence are sound. The classical PDG gives a
wrong slice for \numCpdgUnsound{} of the \numNPairs{} pairs, or \numCpdgUnsoundPct{}. Its control
dependence matters on dynamic circuits, where it is wrong on \numCpdgDynamicUnsound{} pair against
\numDuDynamicUnsound{} for def-use, which ignores classical bits. On the unitary circuits the two rules
are identical, and both are wrong on \numCpdgUnitaryUnsound{} pairs. Of the \numCpdgMissTotal{}
dependences the classical PDG misses, \numCpdgMissKickback{} reach the criterion only through a gate on
which the criterion qubit is a control, the phase kickback that Proposition~\ref{prop:du} illustrates, and
only \numCpdgMissFeedback{} reach it through classical feedback. The confirmatory test H1 confirms that
state-based pruning, which starts from the wire slice, is wrong on fewer pairs than def-use
(\numRqOneQUnsound{} against \numRqOneDuUnsound{}, exact McNemar, Holm \numHOnePHolm{}).

The wire rule is sound on every pair and recovers every dependence, as Theorem~\ref{thm:sound} and
Proposition~\ref{prop:complete} predict. Entanglement closure is also sound, but its added edges bring in
operations that are not dependences. Its precision falls from \numRqOneLcPrecision{} to
\numRqOneYzPrecision{}, and its slices grow from \numRqOneLcSize{} to \numRqOneYzSize{} of the circuit. On
the \numFamCircuits{} circuits with known structure, no rule with quantum dependence is ever wrong, while
the classical PDG is wrong on \numFamCpdgUnsound{} of their pairs. The \numCatEntries{} catalog cases
agree with the hand-derived expectations for def-use, the wire rule, entanglement closure and state-based
pruning, and commutation pruning is sound and within the wire slice on every case where Qiskit's pass
applies.

Slice sizes should be read against the dependence set. The dependences of a qubit make up \numDepFloor{}
of its circuit on average, so no dependence-complete slice can be smaller, and the wire slice keeps only
\numLcExcess{} percentage points more. It equals the dependence set exactly on \numLcExact{} of the
\numNPairs{} pairs, and on \numPairsAllDep{} pairs every operation of the circuit is a dependence.
Entanglement closure keeps \numYzExcess{} points more than the dependence set. The sizes are large because
most benchmark circuits are small and densely coupled. As Fig.~\ref{fig:size} shows, slices shrink as
circuits widen and less of a circuit can reach one qubit. At 11 to 14 qubits the wire slice keeps
\numWElevenLc{} of the circuit. On corpus~L, most of whose circuits come from VeriQBench, it keeps
\numCorpusLSizeLc{}, so width alone does not set the size. Entanglement closure stays near
\numCorpusLSizeYz{}, because it links the operations of a whole coupling component however wide the
circuit is. Commutation pruning falls below the dependence set, to \numWElevenLcq{} at 11 to 14 qubits,
because it is sound at the tested inputs but not dependence-complete, as RQ2 explains.

\subsection{RQ2: How much precision beyond the wire slice is safe?}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_robust.pdf}
  \caption{Share of pairs whose slice, computed at $\ket{0\dots0}$, stays correct in all \numRobDraws{}
  draws of each input regime, for RQ2. Commutation is shown on the \numLcqApplicable{} pairs where Qiskit's
  pass applies, state-based pruning on the \numHThreePrunedN{} pairs it prunes, and angle rebinding on the
  pairs of circuits with rotation angles.}
  \label{fig:robust}
\end{figure}

Both refinements make slices smaller, but only commutation pruning keeps them correct. State-based pruning
shrinks the wire slice by \numHTwoDiff{} percentage points of the circuit (95\% CI \numHTwoCILow{} to
\numHTwoCIHigh{}), a relative reduction of \numHTwoRelative{}, and is smaller on \numHTwoSmaller{} pairs
(H2, Wilcoxon, Holm \numHTwoPHolm{}). Fig.~\ref{fig:robust} shows what that costs. Of the pairs it
prunes, the slice stays correct for all random product inputs on only \numHThreePrunedRate{}, and over
all pairs on \numHThreeRate{} (Wilson CI \numHThreeCILow{} to \numHThreeCIHigh{}), far below the
pre-registered threshold of 95\%, so H3 fails. A state-based slice is therefore a property of one input,
not of the program.

Commutation pruning is smaller than the wire slice on \numLcqSmallerThanLc{} of \numLcqApplicable{} pairs
and stays correct on every random basis and product input. It fails only when the angles change, on
\numRobLcqParamFail{} of \numRobLcqParamN{} pairs of variational circuits whose gates commute with the
criterion's observables at their written angle. The symbolic variant removes these failures. It stays
correct on all \numLcqsBasisN{} pairs under basis and product inputs and on all \numLcqsParamN{} under
angle rebinding, at a mean size of \numLcqsSize{} against \numLcqSize{}. State-based slices are only
\numQVsLcqReduction{} smaller than commutation-pruned slices on average, and larger on
\numQLargerThanLcq{} pairs. Commutation pruning misses dependences, with recall \numLcqRecall{}, because
it drops a gate that commutes with the observables, although a different gate at that position could
change the criterion.

\subsection{RQ3: What does each definition do to fault localization?}

The wire slice of the faulty circuit contains the fault site in \numRqThreeContainLc{} of the
\numRqThreeUnits{} units, against \numRqThreeContainCpdg{} for the classical PDG,
\numRqThreeContainQ{} for state-based pruning, and \numRqThreeContainRandom{} for a random set of the
state-based slice's size. All \numLcMissTotal{} units the wire slice misses are omission faults, a
removed control (\numLcMissCrm{}), a re-targeted operand (\numLcMissQsw{}), a removed preparation of an
entangling gate (\numLcMissEnt{}) or a deleted gate (\numLcMissDel{}). These faults delete the interaction
that a slice of the faulty program would have to follow, a limit of slicing any faulty program rather
than of the rule.

State-based pruning changes containment by \numHFourDiff{} percentage points against the wire slice
(95\% CI \numHFourCILow{} to \numHFourCIHigh{}), so H4, which allowed a loss of at most five points, fails.
In \numCQFour{} units the wire slice contains the fault and the state-based slice does not. Pruning removes
an operation when the criterion is uncorrelated with its qubits at the input, so a fault that breaks a
correlation can make the faulty operation look irrelevant to the single criterion qubit. A joint criterion
over all wrong qubits recovers \numCQFourRecovered{} of these units. Where both slices contain the fault,
the state-based slice is smaller by \numHFiveSizeDiff{} operations on average, so H5 holds. The fault is
ranked no later there, because the state-based slice is a subset of the wire slice, but its EXAM score is
higher by \numHFiveExamQHigher{}. With mutation-based fault localization on \numMbflUnits{} units, the mean
EXAM score is \numMbflExamAll{} over the whole circuit, \numMbflExamLc{} within the wire slice and
\numMbflExamQ{} within the state-based slice, each slice scored on the \numMbflNLc{} and \numMbflNQ{}
units whose slice contains the fault. Neither slice improves the normalized ranking.

Fig.~\ref{fig:llm} shows that language models localize faults less often from state-based slices. Claude
Sonnet~5.5 finds the fault in its top three for \numTTwoSonnetFull{} of the units given the whole circuit,
\numTTwoSonnetLc{} given the wire slice and \numTTwoSonnetQ{} given the state-based slice. Against the
whole circuit the state-based slice differs by \numTTwoSonnetQVsFullDiff{} percentage points (95\% CI
\numTTwoSonnetQVsFullCI{}, exact McNemar \numTTwoSonnetQVsFullP{}), and against the wire slice by
\numTTwoSonnetQVsLcDiff{} points (CI \numTTwoSonnetQVsLcCI{}, \numTTwoSonnetQVsLcP{}). Haiku~5.5 shows the
same order. For Qwen2.5-Coder-32B the wire slice and the whole circuit are level, at \numTTwoQwenMidLc{}
and \numTTwoQwenMidFull{}, and the state-based slice is lower, at \numTTwoQwenMidQ{}. The 1.5B model
localizes too few faults for the input to matter. No state-based slice beats the whole circuit for any
model. Slicing does not hand a model information it lacks, and it can remove the fault.

On the real Bugs4Q programs, the wire slices keep every faulty line, as do the whole programs, and keep
\numLinesLcMean{} of \numLinesFullMean{} lines on average over \numLinesUnits{} units. These programs are
small. Def-use loses the faulty line in \numLinesDuMissed{} of these units, and also in the phase-only unit
of the bug of Section~\ref{sec:example}, where its slice of $q_0$ omits line~6.

\subsection{RQ4: What does it cost?}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_cost.pdf}
  \caption{Median time to slice one criterion and to build the graph of entanglement closure, for circuits
  of width 8 and growing depth, for RQ4. At the two largest depths, only the criteria that finished within
  the limit are counted, so the state-based time is a lower bound.}
  \label{fig:cost}
\end{figure}

As Fig.~\ref{fig:cost} shows, the wire slice costs milliseconds at every depth, at most
\numCostLcMaxCritMs{}~ms per criterion. State-based pruning simulates the circuit for every candidate
removal. Its time grows with depth and exceeds the \numCostWallLimit{}~s limit for
\numCostDepthEightyOverLimit{} of the five circuits at the second-largest depth and for all
\numCostDepthOneSixtyOverLimit{} at the largest. It also grows exponentially with width, reaching
\numCostWidthQMax{}~s at 14 qubits. On corpus~L it completes on \numCorpusLCompleted{} of
\numCorpusLCircuits{} circuits of 15 to 20 qubits. The graph of entanglement closure is itself costly,
taking \numCostBuildDepthForty{}~s to build at depth 40 and \numCostBuildDepthOneSixty{}~s at depth 160 in
the median, because the closure links every pair of operations on a coupling component, while the wire
rule needs only the per-qubit edges.
