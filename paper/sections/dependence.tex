\section{Quantum Dependence and Slicing}\label{sec:dependence}

This section defines what a slice of a quantum circuit must preserve, what an operation's dependence
on a qubit is, the dependence rules we compare, the kinds of slices that follow from them, and the slice
profile of a qubit. The definitions are independent of any slicer, and the evaluation in
Section~\ref{sec:results} measures each rule against them.

\subsection{Circuits and their semantics}
A circuit $P = o_0 \dots o_{L-1}$ is a sequence of operations over qubits $Q = \{0, \dots, n-1\}$ and
classical bits $C$. Each operation $o$ acts on a set of \emph{locations} $\mathit{loc}(o) \subseteq
Q \cup C$. A gate acts on its qubits, a measurement on its qubit and the bit it writes, a reset on its
qubit, and a classically conditioned gate on its qubits and the bits its condition reads. For an input
state $\rho$ of the qubits, with every bit initially $0$, $\llbracket P \rrbracket(\rho)$ denotes the
unconditional final state, that is, the joint state of qubits and bits averaged over measurement
outcomes. For $W \subseteq Q$ and $\overline{W} = (Q \setminus W) \cup C$,
$\rho_W = \operatorname{Tr}_{\overline{W}}(\rho)$ is the reduced state of $W$, the state of the qubits in
$W$ alone after averaging over the other qubits and the bits. It determines every
measurement statistic of $W$. For a set of positions $S$, the restriction $P|_S$ keeps the operations at
the positions in $S$, in their original order. Every slicer in this paper returns such a set, and every
slice is judged by running $P|_S$.

\subsection{Criteria, slices and dependence}

\begin{definition}[Slicing criterion]\label{def:criterion}
A slicing criterion is a nonempty set $W \subseteq Q$ of qubits, whose state is taken at the end of the
circuit, before any terminal measurement reads it.
\end{definition}

Taking the state before readout makes a slice correct also when it is composed with later gates or read in
another basis. A terminal measurement of $W$ sees only the part of this state in the computational basis,
and Section~\ref{sec:results} reports errors in both senses. A criterion at an intermediate point is the
criterion of the prefix of the circuit up to that point, and criteria over classical bits are left to the
roadmap of Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

Yu and Zhao keep the classical criterion $(s, V)$ unchanged~\cite{yu2025qpdg}. A single qubit has no
value of its own, however. Its state is determined only together with the qubits it is correlated with.
We therefore let the criterion be a set of qubits and preserve their \emph{joint} state. $W = \{q\}$ is
the single-qubit criterion.

\begin{definition}[State preservation and soundness]\label{def:sound}
A set of positions $S$ \emph{preserves} $W$ at input $\rho$ if
$\operatorname{Tr}_{\overline{W}}\llbracket P|_S \rrbracket(\rho) =
\operatorname{Tr}_{\overline{W}}\llbracket P \rrbracket(\rho)$. $S$ is a \emph{sound} slice for $W$ if
it preserves $W$ at every input.
\end{definition}

Comparing reduced states is basis-independent. It detects phase errors that a measured distribution in
one basis misses.

Soundness says that the slice reproduces what the criterion qubits end up in. A developer localizing a
fault needs a second guarantee, that the slice contains every place where a fault could change that
outcome.

\begin{definition}[Quantum dependence]\label{def:dep}
Operation $o_k$ is a \emph{dependence} of $W$ in $P$ if there exist an input $\rho$ and a unitary $U$ on
the qubits of $o_k$ such that inserting $U$ immediately after $o_k$ changes
$\operatorname{Tr}_{\overline{W}}\llbracket P \rrbracket(\rho)$. For a measurement, inserting a classical
negation of the bit it writes also counts as such a change.
$\mathit{Dep}_W(P)$ is the set of dependences of $W$. A set $S$ is \emph{dependence-complete} for $W$ if
$\mathit{Dep}_W(P) \subseteq S$.
\end{definition}

This is semantic dependence in the sense of Podgurski and Clarke~\cite{podgurski1990formal}, stated for
quantum states.

A unitary is any reversible gate. The two properties are distinct. Deleting a unitary
$o_k \notin \mathit{Dep}_W(P)$ leaves $\rho_W$ unchanged, as inserting $U = o_k^{\dagger}$ shows. A sound
slice, conversely, need not contain every dependence. The ground truth of Section~\ref{sec:design} approximates the existential quantifiers of
Definition~\ref{def:dep} by sampled inputs and perturbations, so the measured $\mathit{Dep}_W(P)$ is a
subset of the true one.

\subsection{Dependence rules}

A \emph{dependence rule} $R$ assigns each operation the locations it reads, $r_R(o)$, and the
locations it writes, $w_R(o)$. Operation $o_j$ depends on $o_i$ under $R$ if $i < j$ and some location
in $w_R(o_i) \cap r_R(o_j)$ is not written by any operation between them. The backward slice
$B_R(W)$ is the set of operations from which the last writer of some $q \in W$ is reachable. A rule can
account for three kinds of dependence. \emph{Data} dependence follows a qubit from the operation that
last changed it. \emph{Control} dependence follows a classical bit from the measurement that wrote it to
the operations its value conditions. \emph{Quantum} dependence lets a multi-qubit gate change every qubit
it acts on, including its controls, as phase kickback does. It is neither entanglement nor superposition,
and Proposition~\ref{prop:ent} and the end of this section show that neither decides dependence. We compare three rules that differ in which of these they include,
and two refinements of the most complete one.

The \emph{classical PDG}, written CPDG in subscripts, applies the classical program dependence
graph~\cite{ferrante1987pdg} to the circuit, with data and control dependence. Its data dependence reads
a gate as a classical assignment. A controlled gate reads its controls and targets and writes only its
targets, and any other gate reads and writes all its qubits. Its control dependence follows the classical
bits. A measurement writes its bit, and a conditioned gate reads the bits its condition tests. We also
report \emph{def-use} (DU), the same data dependence without classical bits, as the simplest transfer of
classical slicing.

The \emph{quantum dependence graph} (QDG) adds quantum dependence to the classical PDG. Its nodes are
the operations, and every operation reads and writes all its locations,
$r_{\mathrm{QDG}}(o) = w_{\mathrm{QDG}}(o) = \mathit{loc}(o)$, so a controlled gate also writes its
controls. Its edges are the resulting data, control and quantum dependences. The QDG slice $\Bw(W)$ is
backward reachability in the QDG from $W$. It is the backward light cone of $W$, the operations from which
a chain of shared qubits reaches $W$, extended along classical bits from measurements to the gates they
condition.

The \emph{QPDG} is our reimplementation of Yu and Zhao's graph~\cite{yu2025qpdg} as a baseline. Its
dependences are given in prose, so we logged every choice before any run. Data and control dependence are
implemented as in the QDG, with every gate writing all its qubits. Entanglement dependence needs a rule for
when two qubits are entangled, which the QPDG does not give. We use the flow-insensitive rule of entanglement
analysis, under which qubits joined by a two-qubit gate anywhere in the circuit are entangled, and add an
edge from every operation to every later one on qubits of the same such component. Its no-cloning and
temporal dependences add no edge, as the end of this section explains. A flow-sensitive variant counts two
qubits as entangled only from the gate that couples them on. Under this reading the QPDG is the QDG plus
entanglement edges, so its slices contain those of the QDG, and the purple operations of
Fig.~\ref{fig:ansatz}c are what the extra edges cost. Yu and Zhao do not say whether a gate writes its
control, so we also run the reading in which it does not. Table~\ref{tab:rules} lists the rules.

\emph{Commutation pruning} removes an operation of $\Bw(W)$ that commutes with all of the Pauli
observables $X$, $Y$ and $Z$ of all criterion qubits, propagated backward through the circuit, and keeps
the others. An operation that commutes with all of them cannot change a measurement statistic of the
criterion. Qiskit's
\texttt{LightCone} transpiler pass implements this~\cite{qiskit2025}.

\emph{State-based pruning} starts from $\Bw(W)$ at a given input, by default $\ket{0\dots0}$, in which
every qubit is $0$. It removes an operation that does not act on the criterion if the mutual information
between the criterion and the operation's qubits, a measure of how correlated they are, is below
$10^{-9}$ and the removal still preserves $W$ at that input.

\subsection{Properties}

\begin{theorem}[The QDG is sound]\label{thm:sound}
For every circuit $P$ and criterion $W$, $\Bw(W)$ is a sound slice for $W$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{IEEEproof}
Process $P$ backward with a set of needed locations $N$, initially $W$. Operation $o_k$ joins the slice,
and $N$ grows by $\mathit{loc}(o_k)$, exactly when $\mathit{loc}(o_k) \cap N \neq \emptyset$. This
computes $\Bw(W)$. Every operation is a channel on the qubits and bits it acts on. A gate is a unitary
channel on its qubits, a reset replaces the state of its qubit, a measurement dephases its qubit and writes
the outcome into its bit, and a gate conditioned on bits applies a channel controlled by their classical
value, so each acts only on $\mathit{loc}(o)$. An operation outside the slice acts only on locations outside the current $N$,
and by locality of the partial trace, the no-signaling principle, it cannot change the reduced state of the
others, so removing it preserves the reduced state of the needed locations at that point, for every input.
An operation in the slice acts within the needed set it leaves behind, so the reduced state of that set
afterward depends only on its reduced state before. Induction over $P$ gives the claim for $W$. The
induction is mechanized in the Isabelle/HOL proof assistant for an abstract model of local operations whose
two locality assumptions are shown satisfiable. That quantum operations satisfy them is the argument above,
which is not mechanized.
\end{IEEEproof}

\begin{proposition}[The QDG is dependence-complete]\label{prop:complete}
$\mathit{Dep}_W(P) \subseteq \Bw(W)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{IEEEproof}
Let $o_k \notin \Bw(W)$, and let $P'$ insert $U$ after $o_k$. If $U$ were in $\Bw(W)$ for $P'$,
then $o_k$, which precedes $U$ on the same locations, would be in $\Bw(W)$ for $P$. So $P'$ and $P$
have the same QDG slice, and by Theorem~\ref{thm:sound} both preserve $W$. Hence
$\operatorname{Tr}_{\overline{W}}\llbracket P' \rrbracket(\rho) =
\operatorname{Tr}_{\overline{W}}\llbracket P|_{\Bw(W)} \rrbracket(\rho) =
\operatorname{Tr}_{\overline{W}}\llbracket P \rrbracket(\rho)$. The argument for a negated
measurement outcome is the same.
\end{IEEEproof}

Fig.~\ref{fig:ansatz} shows where the other rules fail, on one program that needs both control and
quantum dependence. An ansatz is the parameterized circuit of a variational algorithm such as the
variational quantum eigensolver (VQE), whose rotation angles a classical optimizer tunes. The ansatz of
Fig.~\ref{fig:ansatz}a has layers of rotations and a chain of CNOTs, and after them an auxiliary qubit is
measured mid-circuit and $q_0$ is flipped when the outcome is $1$, on lines~17 to~20.

Control dependence is needed whenever measurement outcomes steer later gates. Def-use ignores the
classical bits, so its slice of $q_0$ keeps only \numAnsDuOps{} of the \numAnsOps{} operations, with
fidelity \numAnsDuFidelity{}, and loses the auxiliary's gate and measurement, which the classical PDG
reaches through the control edge of Fig.~\ref{fig:ansatz}b.

\begin{proposition}[The classical PDG is unsound]\label{prop:du}
There are circuits for which $B_{\mathrm{CPDG}}(W)$, and $B_{\mathrm{DU}}(W)$, which equals it on unitary circuits, do not preserve $W$
even at $\ket{0\dots0}$.
\end{proposition}

Control dependence is not enough, however. Each \texttt{cx(qubit, qubit+1)} of the ansatz acts on its
control as well as its target. A controlled operation acts coherently on a superposition of
its controls~\cite{svore2018qsharp}, so its control is not a classical read. Through these gates the
state of $q_0$ comes to depend on the rotations of $q_1$ and, from the second layer on, of $q_2$. This is
the action of a controlled gate on its control, of which phase kickback is the simplest case.
The classical PDG treats a control as read only, so its slice of $q_0$ keeps only the rotations on $q_0$
itself and the dynamic step, the \numAnsCpdgOps{} shaded nodes of Fig.~\ref{fig:ansatz}b, and misses
\numAnsCpdgMissed{} dependences, with fidelity \numAnsCpdgFidelity{}. The edges it lacks, drawn in orange, are exactly the quantum edges of
Fig.~\ref{fig:ansatz}c. Control dependence cannot repair this, because the missing dependence is quantum.
The QDG slice keeps the \numAnsWireOps{} operations that are dependences of $q_0$, and no other.

\begin{proposition}[Entanglement is neither necessary nor sufficient for dependence]\label{prop:ent}
(i) An operation can be a dependence of $q$ although, at an input that witnesses the dependence, $q$ is
never entangled with its qubits.
(ii) An operation can act on a qubit entangled with $q$ and not be a dependence of $q$.
\end{proposition}

For (i), at input $\ket{0\dots0}$, $q_0$ in the program of Fig.~\ref{fig:bv} is in a pure state after
every operation, in the buggy version and the fixed one, so it is never entangled with anything. Yet the gates of lines~24
and~25 on the auxiliary and the CNOT of line~40 are dependences of $q_0$, since restoring $Z$ on line~25
changes the state of $q_0$. For (ii), in
$\mathrm{H}(q_0)\,\mathrm{CX}(q_0, q_1)\,\mathrm{X}(q_1)$ the two qubits are maximally entangled, but
$\mathrm{X}(q_1)$ is not a dependence of $q_0$. By no-signaling, no operation on other qubits can change
$\rho_{q_0}$. By Proposition~\ref{prop:complete}, the entanglement edges of the QPDG add no dependence
that the QDG misses, and (ii) shows that they can add operations that are not dependences. In
Fig.~\ref{fig:ansatz}c the QPDG adds the \numAnsYzExtra{} purple
operations of $q_2$ in the second layer, which run after the last gate that carries information from $q_2$
to $q_0$.

\begin{proposition}[The QDG distributes over criteria]\label{prop:joint}
$\Bw(W_1 \cup W_2) = \Bw(W_1) \cup \Bw(W_2)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{IEEEproof}
By induction over the backward pass of Theorem~\ref{thm:sound}, the needed set for $W_1 \cup W_2$ is
the union of the needed sets for $W_1$ and $W_2$ at every position.
\end{IEEEproof}

\paragraph*{What is not a dependence}
Entanglement does not decide dependence, by Proposition~\ref{prop:ent}. Nor do the other quantum
phenomena that have been proposed as sources of dependence. No-cloning forbids copying an unknown state,
and a CNOT that copies a basis state is a gate on two qubits, whose edges data dependence already has.
Timing and scheduling decide when an operation runs on hardware, not which operations a state depends on,
and they are not part of a circuit. None of the rules depends on amplitudes either. Superposition changes amplitudes, not which qubits an operation
acts on~\cite{nielsen2010quantum}, and a dependence graph has one node per operation and edges between
operations on shared locations, so superposition cannot enlarge it. Superposition matters only for
state-based pruning, whose checks simulate the circuit and cost time exponential in its width, as
Section~\ref{sec:results} shows.

\subsection{Kinds of slices}
The backward slice answers which operations determine the criterion. Classical slicing knows other kinds
of slices~\cite{tip1994a, silva2012vocabulary}, and each carries over to quantum programs differently.

\paragraph*{Forward slices}
The forward slice $\Fw(o_k)$ of an operation is the set of operations reachable from $o_k$ in the QDG. It
answers the question of a developer who changes a gate, namely which later operations and which qubits the
change can reach. We say that $o_k$ reaches $q$ if $\Fw(o_k)$ contains the last operation on $q$.

\begin{proposition}[Forward and backward slices are dual]\label{prop:dual}
For every operation $o_k$ and qubit $q$, $o_k$ reaches $q$ if and only if $o_k \in \Bw(\{q\})$. Hence
$o_k$ reaches every qubit of which it is a dependence.
\end{proposition}

\begin{IEEEproof}
Both sides say that the QDG has a path from $o_k$ to the last operation on $q$. The second claim follows
from Proposition~\ref{prop:complete}.
\end{IEEEproof}

\paragraph*{Undirected slices}
Following QDG edges in both directions from the operations on $q$ gives the operations connected to $q$
at all. Such a slice is sound, since it contains $\Bw(\{q\})$, but it also contains every operation that
$q$ later affects and everything those operations depend on. Section~\ref{sec:results} shows that it keeps
almost the whole circuit, so it is a pitfall rather than a decomposition of the program by qubit.

\paragraph*{Joint criteria}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:joint}, the QDG slice of a set of qubits is the union of their single-qubit
slices, so a tool needs to compute only those. The criterion still matters for pruning.
State-based pruning for $\{q\}$ preserves $\rho_q$ but not the correlations of $q$ with the other qubits,
so it can delete an operation that changes $\rho_W$ for a larger $W$.

\paragraph*{Static and conditioned slices}
A sound slice preserves the criterion at every input, as Definition~\ref{def:sound} requires. State-based pruning
tests mutual information and checks the reduced state at one given input, and preserves the criterion only
there, so it computes a conditioned slice in the sense of Canfora et
al.~\cite{canfora1998conditioned}, which is correct for the inputs that satisfy a condition, here a single
input. For one complete input, such a slice is a dynamic slice~\cite{korel1988dynamic}, and deleting
operations and checking the observed behavior is the method of observation-based
slicing~\cite{binkley2014orbs}. Quasi-static slicing, which fixes some of the inputs~\cite{venkatesh1991semantic},
lies in between. A closed program starts in $\ket{0\dots0}$, so the restriction matters when the slice is
reused as part of a larger circuit or its angles are bound anew, as in variational algorithms, the regimes
Section~\ref{sec:results} tests.

\paragraph*{Granularity}
A slice can be reported as operations, as loop iterations, or as source lines. Operations are the unit of
the definitions and lines the unit of the developer. A line is in the slice if one of its operations is,
so a line-level slice can keep code that no operation-level slice needs. In the second layer of the ansatz
of Fig.~\ref{fig:ansatz}, the rotations of $q_2$ are not dependences of $q_0$, yet lines 12 and 13 are in
the slice. The tool therefore also reports the iterations of each line. A slice of operations is an
executable circuit, while a slice of lines marks the code to read and is not meant to run.

\subsection{Qubit slice profiles}
srcSlice computes for every variable a slice profile that collects its definitions, uses, aliases, the
functions it is passed to, and the variables that depend on it~\cite{alomari2014srcslice}. We define the
analogous profile of a qubit $q$. It lists the lines of the operations on $q$, each of which both uses and
defines $q$ under the QDG, the source expressions that denote $q$ in gate calls, the call sites of the
functions inside which $q$ is acted on, the other qubits that the first operation on $q$ reaches,
transitively unlike the direct dependent variables of srcSlice, the bits
measured from $q$ and the lines conditioned on them, and the lines of the backward slice of $q$ and of the
forward slice of its first operation. In the program of Fig.~\ref{fig:bv}, the tool computes the profile of
$q_0$ as $\mathsf{uses}(q_0) = \numBvProfDefsSet{}$, $\mathsf{functions}(q_0) = \emptyset$,
$\mathsf{names}(q_0) = \{{\texttt{i} \mapsto \numBvProfNameISet{}}, {\texttt{q} \mapsto \numBvProfNameQSet{}}\}$,
$\mathsf{dependents}(q_0) = \numBvProfDependentsSet{}$, $\mathsf{bits}(q_0) = \numBvProfBitsSet{}$ with no
conditioned lines, $\mathsf{backward}(q_0) = \numBvProfBackwardSet{}$ and $\mathsf{forward}(q_0) =
\numBvProfForwardSet{}$, where every set except the qubits and bits holds line numbers. A profile gathers
in one view what a developer first asks about a qubit, and it costs one backward and one forward pass.
