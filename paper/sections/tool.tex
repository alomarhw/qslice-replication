\section{\tool: Quantum Slicing on srcML and srcSlice}\label{sec:tool}

A slice helps developers only as lines of their program. \tool{} slices a Qiskit
program~\cite{javadi2024qiskit} or an OpenQASM~2 circuit statically with the QDG of Section~\ref{sec:dependence}. It is built on
srcML~\cite{collard2011lightweight, collard2013srcml} and srcSlice~\cite{alomari2014srcslice}, which
computes its classical lines, and it adds the quantum layer that neither has. Fig.~\ref{fig:tool} shows its steps on the ansatz of
Fig.~\ref{fig:ansatz}.

\subsection{Why srcML and srcSlice}
Four properties matter for a slicer whose output a developer reads. First, srcML parses a file without
compiling or running it. \tool{} therefore slices programs that cannot be run, such as fragments, programs
written for earlier Qiskit releases, as the Bugs4Q programs are, and programs from untrusted sources, and
it needs neither a simulator nor quantum hardware. Second, srcML keeps every character of the source,
including whitespace and comments, and the position of every element, and converts back to source without
loss~\cite{collard2011lightweight, behler2024supporting}. A slice is therefore shown on the developer's own
text, line by line. Third, srcML marks up its languages with one vocabulary of
elements~\cite{collard2013srcml}, and a markup for OpenQASM has been
specified~\cite{behler2024supporting}, so a front end for another language needs only the quantum layer,
and the classical and quantum parts of a polyglot program share one representation~\cite{behler2025static}.
Fourth, slicing on srcML scales. srcSlice computes dependences as needed while it slices, without building
a dependence graph, and gives the lines, dependent variables, aliases and calls of every variable of the
Linux kernel in about 20 minutes, with accuracy comparable to CodeSurfer~\cite{alomari2014srcslice}.
Behler et al.\ proposed adapting srcML's slicer to find every line that affects a
qubit~\cite{behler2025static}, and \tool{} realizes that proposal.

\subsection{What srcML and srcSlice see in a Qiskit program}
srcML marks up a Qiskit program as Python. Behler et al.\ needed a new markup for OpenQASM, whose syntax
srcML could not parse~\cite{behler2024supporting}. Qiskit is ordinary Python, so its syntax needs no new
markup, but its quantum meaning lies in library calls that no markup captures. In stage~1 of
Fig.~\ref{fig:tool}, \texttt{qc.cx(qubit, qubit + 1)} is a \texttt{call} whose arguments are two
expressions. srcSlice sees the same program as variables. Its profile of \texttt{qc} uses every gate line,
because each gate is a call on the one circuit object, and its profile of \texttt{qubit} records that the
variable is passed as the first and the second argument of \texttt{cx}. srcSlice therefore knows which
variables reach which argument of which call, but not which qubits a gate acts on, so its slice of
\texttt{qc} is the whole circuit and it has no slice of a qubit at all. Table~\ref{tab:flaws} lists what
srcSlice lacks for quantum programs and where \tool{} supplies it. \tool{} adds the quantum layer in five
extensions.

\subsection{The quantum extensions}

\paragraph*{E1, quantum roles of calls}
A table of \numToolGateTable{} Qiskit instruction methods, including those of earlier releases, records
which arguments of each are qubits, classical bits and angles, and which leading qubits are controls. It
turns a srcML \texttt{call} element into a gate on qubit operands, as the letters of stage~1 mark. It plays
the part of the quantum argument lists of the OpenQASM markup, for a language in which the roles are not in
the syntax.

\paragraph*{E2, qubits and execution order}
\tool{} turns the srcML tree into an operation trace, as the execution-order analysis of QStatic does for
OpenQASM. Because Qiskit computes its qubits with Python, \tool{} evaluates the
program's expressions over known values, registers, bits and circuits. It unrolls loops of up to 10{,}000 iterations and records the
iteration of each operation, inlines functions, and follows \texttt{if\_test} and \texttt{c\_if}, Qiskit's
constructs for gates conditioned on measured bits, as stage~2 of Fig.~\ref{fig:tool} shows. Where a value
cannot be resolved statically, it over-approximates. An unknown qubit index acts on every qubit it may
denote, an unknown condition runs every branch, and a loop over an unknown or longer range repeats its body once
more than the number of qubits and bits it touches, at most \numToolMaxCopies{} times. A circuit passed to code
\tool{} does not model is reported as not traced.

\paragraph*{E3, quantum dependence}
srcSlice's rule for a statement is that it defines the variables it assigns and uses the others, so
\texttt{qc.cx(a, b)} defines nothing and uses \texttt{qc}, \texttt{a} and \texttt{b}. \tool{} replaces this
rule for qubits with the QDG. A gate defines and uses all its qubits, a measurement defines its bit, and a
conditioned gate uses the bits its condition tests. The slice is the backward pass of
Theorem~\ref{thm:sound} on the trace, which skips barriers and terminal measurements. An operation that
may act on several qubits adds all of them to the needed set, and a bit leaves the needed set only when an
operation certainly writes it, so the over-approximations of E2 only enlarge a slice, and \tool{} reports
each. Like srcSlice, \tool{} computes these dependences while it slices instead of building the graph, in
time linear in the trace. The pass returns the operations of the slice, their lines and iterations, as
stage~3 of Fig.~\ref{fig:tool} shows.

\paragraph*{E4, classical lines from srcSlice}
The lines that compute the qubits, angles, conditions and loop ranges of a slice come from srcSlice's
profiles. The start lines are the slice's quantum lines and the headers of the loops, conditions and
\texttt{with} blocks that enclose them. A profile whose uses hold a start line is seeded, and the closure
adds its definition lines, every profile whose dependent variables hold its name, and, for a parameter at
position $k$ of a function $f$, every profile passed to $f$ at position~$k$. srcSlice's profiles record
forward facts, which variables a variable reaches and where it is passed, and the closure follows them
backward from the start lines. \tool{} runs srcSlice with an
extension for Python, covering keyword arguments, the receiver offset of methods, loop, \texttt{with}, tuple
and comprehension targets, nested scopes and f-strings, and with fixes that apply to every language
srcSlice supports. Two of these fixes came from building \tool{}. srcSlice recorded no use of a variable
inside a subscript, as in \texttt{q[a]}, which is how Qiskit names qubits and how C indexes arrays, and it
recorded no use of a module variable inside a function. The replication package contains the extension as
a patch to the public srcSlice, with regression tests.

\paragraph*{E5, qubit slice profiles}
\tool{} computes the qubit slice profile of Section~\ref{sec:profiles} from one backward and one forward pass
over the trace and the srcML of the gate calls on the qubit, and Table~\ref{tab:profile} sets it beside
srcSlice's profile of a variable~\cite{alomari2014srcslice}. The forward pass marks every later operation that
acts on a reached qubit or reads a reached bit, and on our test programs it agrees with the backward pass as
Proposition~\ref{prop:dual} requires. The forward slice of a qubit's first operation and the backward slice of
the qubit are different slices, and over the
\numProfBugsQubits{}, \numProfTextQubits{} and \numProfLintqQubits{} qubits of the Bugs4Q programs, the
textbook and \numProfLintqSample{} sampled LintQ files, they differ in length for
\numProfBugsFwdNeBwd{}, \numProfTextFwdNeBwd{} and \numProfLintqFwdNeBwd{} of them.

\subsection{Two front ends, Qiskit and OpenQASM}
\tool{} reads Qiskit programs, the language of the Bugs4Q and LintQ corpora~\cite{zhao2023bugs4q,
paltenghi2024lintq}, and OpenQASM~2 circuits, the interchange format that Qiskit
exports and that the benchmark suites of Section~\ref{sec:design} ship in. The two need different amounts of
work. In Qiskit the qubits of a gate are computed by Python, so \tool{} needs all of E1 and E2. In
OpenQASM~2 the qubit arguments are explicit and the statements run in order, so E1 is given by the syntax and E2
reduces to reading the statements. srcML 1.1 has no OpenQASM language, and the OpenQASM markup has no released parser yet, so
QStatic runs on hand-marked-up files~\cite{behler2025static}. \tool{} therefore reads OpenQASM~2 with a line-mapped reader that builds the same operation
trace, and E3 to E5 apply unchanged. When srcML parses OpenQASM, the reader becomes a srcML front end, and
OpenQASM~3, with its loops and subroutines, then gets E2 as Qiskit does. On all \numQasmCircuitsChecked{}
circuits of corpus~A, the reader's trace equals the circuit Qiskit loads and every qubit's slice equals the
study's.

\subsection{Comparison with LintQ and QChecker}
LintQ and QChecker detect bug patterns, and \tool{} shows the code a qubit depends on, so they answer different
questions, what is wrong and where to look. LintQ orders two operations exactly when they share a qubit, which is
the relation of the QDG, but it uses the order for adjacent patterns and unrolls a loop only up to ten
iterations~\cite{paltenghi2024lintq}. QChecker matches calls in the abstract syntax tree and has no model of
qubits or order~\cite{zhao2023qchecker}. Table~\ref{tab:tools} sets them side by side, and three checks on their
own data show what the qubit view adds.

First, five of LintQ's ten analyses concern qubits and bits, operation after measurement, double measurement,
oversized circuit, conditional without measurement and unmeasurable qubits. Of the \numLqQubitTp{} warnings of
these analyses that LintQ's authors confirmed as bugs on LintQ's corpus, \tool{} traces \numLqQubitTraced{}, and on
\numLqQubitExposed{} of them a predicate on its qubit profiles, fixed before the comparison, holds at the warned
line. A developer who reads the slices of the qubits on a warned line reads \numLqReadMedian{} lines in the median,
against \numLqCodeMedian{} code lines in the file. The other five analyses concern API use, such as a deprecated
gate, and are outside a slice. Second, LintQ's ten-iteration bound matters. In \numLqBeyondTenFiles{} of the
\numScaleSOneSliced{} files of its corpus that \tool{} traces, operations are created after the tenth iteration of a
loop, \numLqBeyondTenOpsPct{} of all their operations. Third, on the examples of both papers, transcribed as
programs, \tool{}'s slices show each bug. In LintQ's Fig.~1, the slice of $q_3$ is empty, the oversized circuit,
and the slice of $q_0$ holds the \texttt{ry} after its measurement. In QChecker's Fig.~5, the slice of $q_2$ holds
the measurement of $q_1$ and the \texttt{cx} that follows it on $q_1$. QChecker's authors report that it cannot detect the Bugs4Q bugs that show only as wrong
outputs~\cite{zhao2023qchecker}. Every Bugs4Q unit of Section~\ref{sec:results} changes an
output, and on all \numLinesUnits{} of them the QDG slice keeps the faulty line, so slices reach the class of bugs
that pattern detectors report they cannot.

\subsection{Validation}
On \numToolDevPrograms{} runnable programs that we wrote, of up to \numToolDevMaxOps{} operations, the static
trace equals the circuit Qiskit builds. On \numToolDevSliceEqual{} of them, every slice equals the
circuit-level QDG slice of the study. The remaining program nests conditional blocks, and on
\numToolNestedDraws{} random product inputs the oracle of Section~\ref{sec:design} finds the circuit-level slice
wrong \numToolNestedStudyWrong{} times and \tool{}'s slice never. \tool{}'s line slices equal those of the
study on all \numToolTTwoUnits{} units whose lines the study maps, which include the \numLinesUnits{} units of
Section~\ref{sec:results}. On the Bugs4Q programs of the study, read without being run, the trace equals the
prepared circuit for \numToolTOneExact{} of \numToolTOneBugs{} bugs, and the remaining bug rewrites its
circuit as a DAG, which \tool{} reports. Of the \numToolBugsQiskit{} Qiskit files of Bugs4Q, the
\numToolTextbookQiskit{} notebooks of the Qiskit textbook~\cite{qiskittextbook} and the \numToolLintqQiskit{}
programs of LintQ's corpus~\cite{paltenghi2024lintq}, \numToolBugsCircuit{}, \numToolTextbookCircuit{} and
\numToolLintqCircuit{} yield a circuit, \numToolBugsResolved{} of the Bugs4Q ones without over-approximation.
No Bugs4Q or LintQ program crashes \tool{}, which takes \numToolBugsMedianMs{}~ms per Bugs4Q program in the
median.

The classical lines of E4 are checked against a second, flow-sensitive computation. The interpreter of E2
carries with every value the lines that computed it, along the one path the trace follows. Over the
\numSsQubits{} qubits of \numSsFiles{} files of our programs, Bugs4Q, the textbook and the LintQ sample,
srcSlice's lines contain the interpreter's for \numSsContainPct{} of the qubits, \numSsDevBugsContainPct{}
over our programs and Bugs4Q and \numSsBugsContain{} of \numSsBugsQubits{} on Bugs4Q alone, with recall
\numSsRecall{} and precision \numSsPrecision{} against them, in \numSsMedianMs{}~ms per file in the median.
Before the subscript and module-variable fixes of E4, containment was \numSsBeforeContainPct{} and \numSsBeforeDevBugsContainPct{}, below
the threshold of 95\% that we fixed before the comparison, and the fixes were made after that result. The
remaining misses lie mostly in functions that build a circuit or a value and return it, and in containers
filled by method calls, whose effect srcSlice's forward slicing does not link to the use. Its extra lines are those of a flow-insensitive slicer, such as a definition of an index in
another loop or the header of a function whose parameter carries a qubit. \tool{} reports srcSlice's lines,
and the interpreter's are an option.
