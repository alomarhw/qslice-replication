\paragraph*{Background}
A qubit's state is a superposition
$\alpha\ket{0} + \beta\ket{1}$, and measuring it yields $0$ or $1$ with probabilities $|\alpha|^2$ and
$|\beta|^2$. Gates are reversible operations on qubits. A controlled gate such as the controlled-NOT (CNOT) applies an operation to
its target depending on its control, but it can also change the control. When the target is in a suitable
superposition, a phase is kicked back onto the control, an effect called phase kickback. Two qubits are
entangled when their joint state cannot be written as a state of each qubit alone, and then the outcomes
of measuring them are correlated in a suitable basis. A measurement writes a classical bit that later gates may read as a condition. Qiskit programs build
circuits in Python, with loops, functions and conditions that compute which gates act on which qubits.

\paragraph*{The scenario}
The Bernstein--Vazirani algorithm~\cite{bernstein1997qct} finds a hidden bit string $s$ with a single query
to an oracle that computes the parity of $s \wedge x$. Fig.~\ref{fig:bv} shows the program of the Qiskit
textbook~\cite{qiskittextbook}, with $s = 011$. The algorithm rests on phase kickback. Lines~24 and~25
prepare the auxiliary qubit $q_3$ in $\ket{-}$, a superposition of $0$ and $1$ with opposite signs. Each
CNOT of line~40 has the auxiliary as its target, and on $\ket{-}$ it leaves the target unchanged and flips
the phase of its control instead. The Hadamard gates of line~47 turn these phases into the bits of $s$. We
seeded one fault, a one-token change on line~25 that applies $X$ instead of $Z$. The auxiliary is then left
in $\ket{+}$, on which a CNOT acts as the identity, and the program returns \numBvBuggyOut{} instead of
\numBvFixedOut{}.

The developer sees $q_0$ read $0$ where $s$ says $1$, and slices backward from $q_0$ to see which
statements determine it. A classical slicer follows data and control dependence. It reads
\texttt{cx(q, n)}, the controlled-NOT that flips its target when its control is $1$, as the assignment
$q_n \leftarrow q_n \oplus q$, which reads $q$ and writes only the auxiliary. Its slice of $q_0$ keeps the
Hadamard gates of lines~\numBvCpdgLines{} and the statements that compute their qubits, lines
\numBvCpdgClassical{}, as the left column of Fig.~\ref{fig:bv} shows. The slice even appears to be right.
Its \numBvCpdgOps{} operations give $q_0$ the same state as the whole program, with fidelity
\numBvCpdgFidelity{}, a similarity of quantum states that is $1$ exactly when they are equal, so the slice
gives the developer no reason to look at lines~\numBvMissedLines{}, where the fault is.

These lines are nonetheless dependences of $q_0$. Restoring $Z$ on line~25 turns $q_0$ from $\ket{0}$ into
$\ket{1}$, two states with fidelity \numBvFixFidelity{}. A CNOT acts on its control as well as its target.
The QDG, in which every gate reads and writes all its qubits, keeps lines~\numBvQdgLines{}, exactly the
dependences of $q_0$, together with the statements that compute them, among them the oracle's loop and
condition on lines~35 to~39. Its slice contains the fault.

Here the fault shows in the measured outcomes of $q_0$. That is not always so. In a real bug from
Bugs4Q~\cite{zhao2023bugs4q}, posted on Stack Overflow, a two-qubit program meant to prepare
$(\ket{01}+\ket{10})/\sqrt{2}$ applies \texttt{h(0)}, \texttt{h(1)} and \texttt{cx(0,1)}, with \texttt{h(1)}
where \texttt{x(1)} was needed, and returns all four bit strings equally often. Each qubit alone reads $1$ with probability \numMotPOne{}, in the buggy program
and in the intended one alike, so a check of the outcomes of either qubit cannot see the failure, and only
their joint outcomes reveal it. The fault does change the state of $q_0$, which is a uniformly random bit
in the intended program and the pure $\ket{+}$ in the buggy one, two states with fidelity
\numMotFidelity{}. A slice judged by the outcome histogram of one
qubit can therefore look right and miss the fault, while a slice judged by the state of the qubit cannot.
Because the CNOT writes its control,
the QDG slice of $q_0$ contains the faulty \texttt{h(1)}, while the classical PDG keeps only \texttt{h(0)}.

Both lessons hold beyond these examples, as Section~\ref{sec:results} shows. Reading a gate as an assignment misses influence that flows into a control qubit,
and judging a slice by the outcomes of one qubit misses errors in phase and in correlations.
