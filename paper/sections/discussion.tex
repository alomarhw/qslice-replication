\section{A Roadmap for Quantum Program Slicing}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph*{What the results support}
The definitions and results suggest how a slicer for quantum programs should be built. The base should be the QDG,
in which every gate reads and writes all its qubits. It is sound for every input, misses no dependence,
and costs milliseconds. Reading a gate as a classical assignment is the pitfall to avoid, and adding
control dependence does not remove it. Entanglement edges, as in the QPDG, should not be added to make a slice safer. They
buy no soundness that the QDG lacks and cost precision, because a later operation on an entangled partner
cannot change the criterion's state, as Proposition~\ref{prop:ent} shows. They contain a few more faults of
a faulty program only by keeping more of it. No dependence slice of the faulty program can show an
omitted interaction, and every fault the QDG slice misses removes one, so a slicer should warn that a
fault may be a missing gate or control that no slice can contain. Whether
slices shorten debugging remains a hypothesis, since our localization results do not show it. Precision beyond the
QDG slice can come from commutation pruning, which stays correct on every random input but fails when the
angles change. Treating every angle as a symbol before the pass, an exploratory variant, kept it correct under all three
input regimes, so a slicer for variational programs should use the symbolic variant or recompute commutation
after each parameter update. State-based pruning should be offered, if at all, as a view of one input,
labeled as such, and never as the slice of the program. Finally, a slicer should judge slices by states rather than by
histograms, since \numFdCpdgZMatchPct{} of the wrong classical-PDG slices pass a histogram check, and it
should let the developer name a set of qubits whose joint state pruning must preserve. By
Proposition~\ref{prop:joint}, the joint QDG slice needs no extra analysis.

\paragraph*{What is open}
Six problems remain, and each extends a classical line of work. The first is precision that holds for
every input. The QDG is sound for all inputs and the dependence set is smaller, and closing that gap statically
needs analyses that reason about states, such as a commutation analysis that is valid for all angles or an
abstract interpretation of stabilizer states. The second is slices for classes of inputs. Conditioned and
quasi-static slicing~\cite{canfora1998conditioned, venkatesh1991semantic} suggest slices that are sound for
a stated class, such as basis states or product states, rather than for one input or for all. The third is
hybrid programs. Variational algorithms loop between a classical optimizer and a circuit, and a slice that crosses
this boundary needs interprocedural dependence through the measured results~\cite{horwitz1990interprocedural}.
The fourth is the remaining kinds of slices and criteria. Chopping between two
operations~\cite{jackson1994chopping}, dicing of a correct and a wrong criterion~\cite{lyle1987dicing},
amorphous slices that may rewrite gates~\cite{harman1997amorphous}, for instance by merging rotations,
criteria over classical bits, and criteria given by an observable rather than a reduced state all need
definitions against the semantics of Section~\ref{sec:dependence}. The fifth is other languages and
compiled code. srcML's markup for OpenQASM~\cite{behler2024supporting, behler2025static} gives a front end,
and a slice of a transpiled circuit must be mapped back to the source through the compiler's layout.
The sixth is evidence with developers. Our fault-localization results use seeded faults and the small programs of
Bugs4Q, and a study of developers debugging real quantum programs with slices is the next step, as it was
for classical slicing~\cite{weiser1982programmers}.

\paragraph*{Noise}
Noise cannot change which operations a slice contains, only whether running it shows what the whole
program would. In a pre-registered descriptive analysis under depolarizing noise at two-qubit error rates
of $10^{-3}$ to $3\cdot10^{-2}$, on the pairs of at most ten qubits, the criterion's noisy states under the
QDG slice and the whole circuit differ in trace distance by at most \numNoiseLcCrossExactMax{} where the
simulation is exact and by at most \numNoiseLcCrossShotMax{} where mid-circuit measurements force
sampling, which is rounding and sampling error. Theorem~\ref{thm:sound} predicts this, since such noise
acts only on the qubits of its gate. The state-based slice
differs from the whole circuit in \numNoiseQCrossExactNonzero{} of \numNoiseQCrossExactN{} exact runs, by
up to \numNoiseQCrossExactMax{}. It removes noisy gates that the whole program executes, and errors
accumulate with the number of gates~\cite{preskill2018nisq}, so it can hide noise the developer is trying
to diagnose. Noise that is not local to a gate, such as crosstalk, is outside this argument.
