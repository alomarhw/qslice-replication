\section{The Approach}\label{sec:b-approach}

The approach adds a quantum layer to srcML and srcSlice. srcML parses the program, and five extensions, E1 to E5,
give its markup a quantum meaning: they read what each call does to which qubits (E1), reconstruct the circuit the
program builds (E2), slice every qubit of that circuit (E3), add the classical statements that compute each slice (E4),
and summarize each qubit in a profile (E5). The result is shown on the program's lines. We implement the approach in a
tool, \tool{}, for Qiskit programs~\cite{javadi2024qiskit} and OpenQASM~2 circuits. Fig.~\ref{fig:b-architecture} shows
its parts and where each comes from, and Fig.~\ref{fig:b-tool} follows the program of Fig.~\ref{fig:b-example} through
them.

\subsection{Why srcML and srcSlice}
A slicer whose output a developer reads needs four properties that srcML and srcSlice already have. srcML parses a file
as text and never compiles or runs it~\cite{collard2011lightweight}, so the approach accepts fragments, programs
written for Qiskit releases that no longer install, and code from sources one would not execute, and it needs neither
a simulator nor a quantum device. srcML keeps every character of the source, layout and comments included, with the
position of each element, and converts its markup back to the exact text~\cite{collard2011lightweight}, so a slice can
be shown on the developer's own lines. srcML marks up all its languages with one vocabulary~\cite{collard2013srcml}, and
a markup for OpenQASM has been specified~\cite{behler2024supporting}, so a new front end needs only its quantum layer
and the classical and quantum parts of a program can share one representation. Last, srcSlice slices from per-variable
profiles that it computes on demand rather than from a dependence graph, which let it process the Linux kernel in about
20 minutes with accuracy comparable to CodeSurfer~\cite{alomari2014srcslice}. Behler et al.\ proposed adapting srcML's
slicer to find the lines that affect a qubit~\cite{behler2025static}, and the approach carries out that proposal for
Qiskit.

\subsection{What srcML and srcSlice see in a Qiskit program}
Qiskit is ordinary Python, so unlike OpenQASM it needs no new markup, but its quantum meaning lies in library calls that
no markup captures. In stage~1 of Fig.~\ref{fig:b-tool}, \texttt{qc.cx(q[src], q[a])} is a \texttt{call} with two
argument expressions. srcSlice sees the program as variables. Its profile of \texttt{qc} uses every gate line, because
every gate is a call on the one circuit object, and its profile of the register \texttt{q} records that it reaches both
arguments of \texttt{cx}. srcSlice therefore knows which variables reach which argument of which call, but not
which qubits a gate acts on, so its slice of \texttt{qc} is the whole program and no qubit has a slice at all.

\subsection{E1, quantum roles of calls}
Qiskit applies a gate through a method of the circuit, as in \texttt{qc.cx(a, b)} or \texttt{qc.ry(theta, q)}. E1
decides, for every such call, which arguments are qubits, which are classical bits and which are angles, and which
leading qubits are controls. It reads this from a role table derived from the signatures of Qiskit's
\texttt{QuantumCircuit} methods rather than from a hand-written list, so the table follows the library. It holds the
\numToolGateQiskit{} methods of Qiskit~\numQiskitVersion{} that take qubits and add an operation, \numToolGateLegacy{}
methods that Qiskit~1.0 removed but older programs still call, and \numToolGateSpecial{} methods whose arguments the
signatures do not name uniformly, \numToolGateTable{} in all. With the table, a srcML \texttt{call} becomes a gate on
qubit operands, as the gate and qubit colours mark in stage~1 of Fig.~\ref{fig:b-tool}, the part the quantum argument lists of the
OpenQASM markup play in a language whose syntax states them. Table~\ref{tab:b-roles} lists the methods by kind, with the
calls outside the table and how they are read. A circuit composed into another is mapped onto the qubits it is given,
and a gate the table does not know, such as a circuit from Qiskit's library, becomes one operation on all its qubits,
which can only enlarge a slice. A method the approach does not model is reported to the developer, never skipped
silently.

\subsection{E2, the static trace}
Qiskit computes qubit indices, register sizes and angles in Python, so E2 interprets the srcML tree of the program over
the values it can know and records the operations the program would append, as a trace, much as the execution-order
analysis of Behler et al.\ does for OpenQASM~3~\cite{behler2025static}. Each operation carries its source line, from
the position srcML records (the grey \texttt{pos:start} in Fig.~\ref{fig:b-tool}), and the iteration of every loop around it. The
interpretation unrolls loops of the host program of up to 10{,}000 iterations, inlines its functions, follows its
branches, and reads \texttt{if\_test} and \texttt{c\_if}, Qiskit's two ways of conditioning gates on measured bits
(the bit and condition colours), as stage~2 of Fig.~\ref{fig:b-tool} shows. Where a value cannot be known without running the program,
it over-approximates. An index it cannot evaluate acts on every qubit it may denote, both branches of a condition it
cannot decide are followed, and a loop of unknown length is repeated as Section~\ref{sec:b-loops} describes, up to
\numToolMaxCopies{} copies. Each over-approximation adds operations or qubits, so it can only enlarge a slice, and each
is reported to the developer, as is a circuit passed to code the interpretation does not model.

\subsection{E3, the slices}
To srcSlice, \texttt{qc.cx(a, b)} defines no variable and uses \texttt{qc}, \texttt{a} and \texttt{b}. E3 reads each
operation of the trace by its qubits and bits instead. A gate writes every qubit it acts on, its controls included, a
measurement writes its bit, and a conditioned operation reads the bits it tests. A qubit's slice is then the backward
light cone of its final state, the relation compilers use to order and place gates~\cite{murali2019triq, amy2020staq},
followed from each measurement to the operations its bit conditions. E3 computes it in one backward pass over the
trace with a set of needed qubits and bits, in time linear in the trace, skipping barriers and the measurements at the
end of the circuit. An operation that may act on several qubits adds all of them to the needed set, and a bit leaves
the set only when an operation certainly writes it, so the over-approximations of E2 stay sound. Like srcSlice, E3
computes dependence while it slices and builds no graph. The pass returns the operations of the slice with their lines
and iterations, as stage~3 of Fig.~\ref{fig:b-tool} shows, and the forward pass from an operation gives its forward
slice. For large circuits, the pass carries the set of criterion qubits on each needed location and so slices every
qubit at once.

\subsection{Loops that run on the device}\label{sec:b-loops}
Since Qiskit~1.0, a circuit can contain its own loops and branches, \texttt{while\_loop}, \texttt{for\_loop},
\texttt{switch} and the \texttt{else} branch of \texttt{if\_test}, which run on the device and test measured bits. A
\texttt{for\_loop} over a known range is unrolled. Any other device loop is repeated, with every operation of its body
conditioned on the bits its condition reads, until a repetition adds no qubit or bit to the needed set.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:b-loops}
Let a device loop with body $B$ end with probability one, let $\Lambda$ be the qubits and bits $B$ acts on, and let the
slice be computed on $|\Lambda| + 1$ conditioned copies of $B$. Deleting from $B$ every operation that none of its copies
puts in the slice of $W$ leaves the state of $W$ unchanged for every input.
\end{proposition}

\begin{IEEEproof}[Proof sketch]
A loop that runs at most $K$ times behaves as $K$ copies of $B$, each conditioned on the bits that decide the loop. In
the backward pass every copy is processed in the same way and the needed set only grows. Once a copy adds nothing, every
earlier copy keeps the same operations, and the needed set can grow at most $|\Lambda|$ times. So for every $K$ the copies
keep only operations that one of the $|\Lambda| + 1$ copies keeps. The bits that decide the loop are read by every copy,
so the measurements that write them stay in the slice and the distribution of the number of iterations is unchanged. A
loop that ends with probability one is the limit of its bounded prefixes.
\end{IEEEproof}

\subsection{E4, the classical statements}
The statements that compute a slice's qubits, angles, conditions and loop bounds come from srcSlice's profiles. E4
starts from the slice's lines and the headers of the loops, conditions and \texttt{with} blocks around them. Every
profile used on a start line is taken, and the closure adds its definition lines, every profile whose dependent
variables name it, and, for a parameter at position $k$ of a function $f$, every profile passed to $f$ at position $k$.
srcSlice's profiles record forward facts, which variables a variable reaches and where it is passed, and E4 follows them
backward from the start lines. srcSlice runs with a patch that adds Python, covering keyword arguments, the receiver
offset of methods, loop, \texttt{with}, tuple and comprehension targets, nested scopes and f-strings, and with fixes that
apply to every language srcSlice supports. Two of these fixes came from this work. srcSlice recorded no use of a
variable inside a subscript, as in \texttt{q[a]}, which is how Qiskit names a qubit and how C indexes an array, and it
missed a module variable read inside a function. The patch applies to the public srcSlice and comes with regression
tests.

\subsection{E5, qubit profiles}
srcSlice records a slice profile for every variable. E5 gives each qubit a counterpart, which gathers what a developer
first asks about it, and Table~\ref{tab:b-profile} sets the two side by side.

\begin{definition}[Qubit profile]\label{def:b-profile}
The profile of a qubit $q$ records its \emph{uses}, the lines of the operations on $q$; its \emph{names}, the source
expressions that denote $q$; its \emph{functions}, the call sites of the functions in which $q$ is acted on; its
\emph{dependents}, the qubits that the first operation on $q$ reaches; its \emph{bits}, those measured from $q$, with the
lines they condition; and its \emph{backward} and \emph{forward} lines, those of its slice and of the forward slice of its
first operation.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Two front ends}
The approach reads Qiskit programs, the language of Bugs4Q and of LintQ's corpus~\cite{zhao2023bugs4q,
paltenghi2024lintq}, and OpenQASM~2 circuits, the format that Qiskit exports and benchmark suites ship in. The two need
different amounts of work. In Qiskit the qubits of a gate are computed in Python, so all of E1 and E2 are needed. In
OpenQASM~2 the qubit arguments are explicit and the statements run in order, so E1 is given by the syntax and E2
reduces to reading the statements. srcML~1.1 has no OpenQASM language, and the OpenQASM markup has no released parser
yet, which is why QStatic runs on hand-marked-up files~\cite{behler2025static}. \tool{} therefore reads OpenQASM~2 with a
reader that maps every operation to its line and produces the same trace, and E3 to E5 apply unchanged. Once srcML
parses OpenQASM, the reader becomes a srcML front end, and OpenQASM~3, with its loops and subroutines, gets E2 as Qiskit
does.
