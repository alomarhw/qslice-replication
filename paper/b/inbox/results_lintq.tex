LintQ and \tool{} answer different questions, what is wrong and where to look, so the comparison asks what the qubit
view adds to LintQ's warnings. Table~\ref{tab:lintq} shows that the profiles contain what LintQ's five qubit analyses
check. Of the \numLqQubitTp{} warnings of these analyses that LintQ's authors confirmed as bugs, \tool{} traces
\numLqQubitTraced{}, and on all of them a predicate on its profiles holds at the warned line. The predicates also hold on
\numLqFpFires{} of the \numLqFpTraced{} traced warnings the authors rejected, and on \numLqSarifFires{} of the
\numLqSarifTraced{} traced warnings in all, so the profiles reproduce these analyses with their false alarms and are not
a more precise detector. What they add is context. LintQ points to one line, and the slices of the qubits on that line
give the \numLqReadMedian{} lines in the median that determine their state, out of \numLqCodeMedian{} code lines in the
file. LintQ's bound of ten loop iterations also matters. In \numLqBeyondTenFiles{} of the programs \tool{} traces,
operations are created after the tenth iteration of a loop, \numLqBeyondTenOpsPct{} of all their operations. On the
examples of both papers, transcribed as programs of our own, the slices show each bug. In LintQ's first example the
slice of $q_3$ is empty, the oversized circuit, and the slice of $q_0$ holds the rotation after its measurement. In
QChecker's fifth example the slice of $q_2$ holds the measurement of $q_1$ and the CNOT that follows it on $q_1$.
